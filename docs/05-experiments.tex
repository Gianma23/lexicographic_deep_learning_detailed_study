\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

In the literature, methods for Hierarchical Image Classification (HIC) are typically evaluated on heterogeneous datasets, differing in scale, hierarchical structure, and overall complexity. This variability makes a direct, like-for-like comparison across approaches difficult.

For example, LH-DNN \cite{fiaschi2024informed} is evaluated mainly on relatively simple datasets such as CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Fashion-MNIST, where the class hierarchy is generally constructed artificially. Capsule-Network-based methods, such as HD-CapsNet \cite{noor2024consistency} and HT-CapsNet \cite{noor2025taxonomy}, employ both synthetic or simplified datasets (e.g., CIFAR and Fashion-MNIST) and fine-grained datasets with natural taxonomies, such as CUB-200-2011 and Stanford Cars. HRN \cite{chen2022label}, one of the earlier CNN-based hierarchical architectures, is evaluated on three widely used fine-grained visual classification (FGVC) benchmarks --- CUB-200-2011, FGVC-Aircraft, and Stanford Cars --- for which the label hierarchy is reconstructed from Wikipedia-derived taxonomies following the procedure of Chang et al. \cite{chang2021flamingo}. More recent Transformer-based approaches, such as H-CAST \cite{park2024visually}, additionally include hierarchical benchmarks derived from ImageNet, namely the BREEDS suite, alongside fine-grained datasets such as CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft. Finally, Hier-COS \cite{sani2025hiercos} evaluates hierarchy-aware representation learning on FGVC-Aircraft and CIFAR-100, together with two large-scale, deeply nested taxonomies: iNaturalist-19 and tieredImageNet-H.

This heterogeneity of benchmarks makes a direct comparison between the different approaches proposed in the literature difficult. For this reason, this work proposes to evaluate the considered architectures on a common set of datasets, in order to obtain a more consistent comparison across methods.


\subsection{Dataset selection}
\label{sec:dataset_selection}

To evaluate the hierarchical classification models considered in this work, a set of benchmarks widely used in the recent literature was selected. Rather than including every dataset present in the analyzed works, a representative subset was chosen, covering different levels of complexity, dataset size, and hierarchy type.

Specifically, the datasets used are \textit{CIFAR-100}, \textit{CUB-200-2011}, and \textit{FGVC-Aircraft}. This selection allows the models to be evaluated across markedly different scenarios: a small, artificially organized hierarchy (CIFAR-100), and fine-grained natural taxonomies with a limited number of training images (CUB-200-2011, FGVC-Aircraft). Table \ref{tab:datasets_info} summarizes the characteristics of the three datasets.

The BREEDS suite was deliberately excluded from this selection, for two reasons. First, being derived from the full ImageNet training set, it imposes computational requirements well beyond those of the other three benchmarks, which is impractical given the computational budget available for this work. Second, and more fundamentally, BREEDS follows a different evaluation paradigm from CIFAR-100, CUB-200-2011, and FGVC-Aircraft: instead of measuring accuracy on a fixed, fully observed taxonomy, each of its four sub-benchmarks (Entity-13, Entity-30, Living-17, Nonliving-26) tests a model's ability to generalize coarse-level (superclass) knowledge to fine-level subclasses that are disjoint between training and test sets, over a two-level hierarchy rather than the three-level structure of the other datasets. Mixing this generalization-to-unseen-subpopulations protocol with the fixed-hierarchy classification protocol of CIFAR-100, CUB-200-2011, and FGVC-Aircraft would undermine the goal, stated above, of obtaining a coherent and directly comparable evaluation across all considered models.

\subsection{Dataset descriptions}
\label{sec:dataset_descriptions}

\subsubsection{CIFAR-100}
CIFAR-100 remains a relevant benchmark, since it still leaves room for improvement both in fine-level classification and in the overall consistency of the hierarchical path. Although recent works have already achieved competitive results, performance is not yet saturated, particularly considering that distinguishing between fine-grained classes remains non-trivial.

\subsubsection{CUB-200-2011}
CUB-200-2011 is one of the datasets with the greatest experimental value, as it allows a direct comparison with H-CAST, HT-CapsNet, and, as discussed below, HRN. H-CAST achieves the best overall results on this benchmark, with a Full-Path Accuracy of 83.28 and an improvement of about 3.2 points over HRN in the configuration reported by the authors \cite{park2024visually}. HT-CapsNet also reports substantial gains on CUB, with absolute improvements exceeding 10\% on some hierarchical metrics relative to the strongest baseline \cite{noor2025taxonomy}. This suggests that the dataset is competitive but not saturated: there remains room for improvement, particularly in balancing fine-grained accuracy against hierarchical consistency.

The three works do not agree on the taxonomy: H-CAST and HRN use a 13/38/200 hierarchy while HT-CapsNet uses a 39/123/200 one. The taxonomy adopted here, and the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives found in the literature, are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}.

\subsubsection{FGVC-Aircraft}
FGVC-Aircraft is particularly interesting because H-CAST clearly improves over hierarchical ViT baselines, yet remains far from the best HRN configuration: H-CAST reports an FPA of 83.72, while HRN reaches 91.39 in the best configuration reported by its authors \cite{park2024visually}. The same authors observe that, on small datasets such as Aircraft, ResNet-based models remain highly competitive \cite{park2024visually}. HT-CapsNet, on the other hand, has not been evaluated on this dataset. Overall, the benchmark is not saturated, but any improvement is likely to require a genuinely competitive method, especially in a fine-grained scenario with a limited number of training images.


\subsection{Hierarchy construction}
\label{sec:hierarchy_construction}

The three selected benchmarks do not share a common notion of hierarchy: each of the label trees used in the experiments has a different origin, and only one of them is published as part of the dataset itself. Since every model in this study is trained against these trees, and since hierarchy-aware metrics such as AHD and TICE are defined \emph{on} them, the provenance of each tree is part of the experimental protocol rather than a detail of data preparation. This subsection therefore states, for each dataset, where every level comes from, which decisions were taken in this work, and what remains uncertain.

Three properties are enforced identically across the three datasets, so that the comparison between models is not confounded by the shape of the label space. First, all hierarchies have exactly three levels, which is the depth required by H-CAST and HRN and the depth at which all considered methods can be run without modification. Second, every class has exactly one parent at every level, so the taxonomy is a tree and not a directed acyclic graph. Third, the label space --- the mapping from raw dataset identifiers to the contiguous class indices seen by the models --- is constructed once from the training split and reused unchanged by validation and test, which prevents split-local relabelling and guarantees that a class index denotes the same concept in every split.

\subsubsection{CIFAR-100: two official levels and one imposed level}

CIFAR-100 \cite{krizhevsky2009learning} publishes two of the three levels. The 100 fine labels are the leaves, and the 20 official superclasses --- which the dataset distributes as \texttt{coarse\_label\_names} --- form the middle level. This mapping is regular by construction: each superclass contains exactly five fine classes.

The dataset provides no third level. The top level used here is the grouping of the 20 superclasses into 8 coarse classes introduced by the B-CNN reference implementation \cite{zhu2017bcnn}, which is adopted so that the CIFAR-100 hierarchy is three levels deep and therefore directly comparable with the other two benchmarks.\footnote{The grouping is taken from the published B-CNN source, \url{https://github.com/zhuxinqimac/B-CNN/blob/main/CIFAR_100_keras_vgg16_hierarchy_dynamic.py}, and is reproduced verbatim in the data adapter of this work.} The resulting eight groups are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), itemsep=0pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item aquatic mammals, fish;
  \item flowers, fruit and vegetables, trees;
  \item food containers, household electrical devices, household furniture;
  \item insects, non-insect invertebrates;
  \item large carnivores, large omnivores and herbivores, medium mammals, reptiles, small mammals;
  \item large man-made outdoor things, large natural outdoor scenes;
  \item people;
  \item vehicles~1, vehicles~2.
\end{enumerate}

Two consequences follow and are carried through the rest of this work. The top level is an \emph{external, manually defined} semantic grouping, not a property of CIFAR-100: results obtained on it describe this particular taxonomy and should not be read as results on ``the'' CIFAR-100 hierarchy. Furthermore, the grouping is deliberately uneven --- the mammal-and-reptile group collects five superclasses while the \emph{people} group collects one --- so CIFAR-100 combines a perfectly regular lower transition with a visibly irregular upper one, a combination that turns out to be useful when interpreting the analysis in Section~\ref{sec:dataset_analysis}.

\subsubsection{CUB-200-2011: a taxonomy inherited from the literature}

CUB-200-2011 \cite{wah2011caltech} publishes 200 species and their images, but no higher taxonomic level. The hierarchy used here is the 13 orders / 38 families / 200 species tree retained from the H-CAST codebase \cite{park2024visually}, stored in this work as an explicit species-to-(order, family) table so that it is versioned together with the experiments rather than reconstructed at load time.

From the H-CAST codebase, the hierarchy can be identified as order/family/species; this was further confirmed by retrieving the actual class names associated with each order, family, and species. Notably, the same 13/38/200 order--family--species hierarchy is independently reported by HRN \cite{chen2022label}, which reconstructs the CUB taxonomy following the procedure of Chang et al. \cite{chang2021flamingo}. The agreement between these two independently obtained hierarchies lends external support to the taxonomy used by H-CAST, and it is the reason this tree was preferred over the alternatives found in the literature.

HT-CapsNet \cite{noor2025taxonomy}, by contrast, adopts a 39/123/200 hierarchy derived from another paper that originally published the corresponding labels through links that are no longer accessible. Moreover, the metrics it reports appear suspiciously low (20--30\%, against roughly 90\% for H-CAST). Further investigation revealed that the paper cited by HT-CapsNet had in turn taken its hierarchy from yet another source. From this latter source, the 39/123/200 split appears to correspond to family/genus/species; however, (i) that paper actually reports a 36/113/200 hierarchy, and (ii) the accuracy it obtains is comparable to that of H-CAST (approximately 80\%). The hierarchy used by HT-CapsNet cannot therefore be considered reliable or independently verifiable, and it was not adopted here.

Additional discrepancies in the H-CAST hierarchy were also flagged during a preliminary, LLM-assisted check; being non-authoritative, this check does not substitute for a rigorous verification. A systematic ground-truth validation of the taxonomic hierarchy actually used --- for instance, by cross-checking every order, family, and species label against an authoritative biological taxonomy database --- therefore remains a necessary next step before this hierarchy can be adopted with full confidence.

\subsubsection{FGVC-Aircraft: the official hierarchy, joined from parallel annotations}

FGVC-Aircraft \cite{maji2013fine} is the only one of the three benchmarks that publishes a complete hierarchy, and it is used here without any external input. The official download distributes the class lists (\texttt{manufacturers.txt}, \texttt{families.txt}, \texttt{variants.txt}) and, for every split, three \emph{parallel} per-image annotation files that label the same images at the manufacturer, family, and variant levels. The tree is therefore not published as a set of parent--child edges: it has to be recovered by joining the three annotation files on the image identifier.

This work performs that join and, in doing so, verifies the assumptions it relies on: the three annotation files of a split are required to cover exactly the same image identifiers; every variant must be observed with a single family and every family with a single manufacturer across the entire dataset, so that the recovered relation is a function; and the resulting parent maps must cover every published class. Violations raise an error rather than being silently repaired. The resulting hierarchy has 30 manufacturers, 70 families, and 100 variants, matching the official class lists.

The official annotation also defines a fourth, finer \emph{model} level, which is not used: three levels are kept for comparability with CIFAR-100 and CUB-200-2011, and the variant level is the one on which the published FGVC-Aircraft results of HRN, H-CAST, and Hier-COS are reported.

\subsubsection{Status of the three taxonomies}

The three trees consequently have different epistemic status, and this asymmetry is a threat to the external validity of any cross-dataset claim made in this work. The FGVC-Aircraft hierarchy is official and fully verifiable from the distributed annotations. The CUB-200-2011 hierarchy is inherited from H-CAST and corroborated by an independent reconstruction in HRN, but it has not yet been validated against an authoritative biological taxonomy. The CIFAR-100 top level is externally imposed and is one grouping among many possible ones. Results are therefore reported for the explicit taxonomies described above, and no claim is made that they transfer to a different hierarchy defined over the same images.


\subsection{Dataset analysis}
\label{sec:dataset_analysis}

This subsection characterizes what the unified protocol actually sees. It has two purposes: to document the splits and the amount of supervision available per class, and to quantify the properties of the label trees that are expected to interact with hierarchical methods --- how unbalanced each tree is, and how much of the fine decision is already determined by the coarse one. All figures and numbers below are produced by the analysis notebook from the same dataset adapters used during training, so split sizes, label indices, and hierarchy edges are identical to those seen by the models.

Throughout this subsection the statements are \emph{descriptive}. They characterize the data and the label space, not the behaviour of any model: no claim about accuracy, hierarchical consistency, or the relative merits of the methods is derived from them here. Those comparisons belong to the results sections of this chapter.

\subsubsection{Splits and supervision per class}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets_info} reports the datasets as they are distributed. The protocol departs from those published partitions in one respect only, and it does so because model selection requires a validation set that is disjoint from the test set. FGVC-Aircraft ships an official train/validation/test partition, which is used unchanged. CIFAR-100 and CUB-200-2011 publish only a train/test partition, so a validation split is carved deterministically out of the published training pool: the split is stratified at the leaf level, uses a fixed seed, and reserves a nominal 10\% of the CIFAR-100 training pool and 15\% of the CUB training pool. Per-class rounding makes the realized CUB fraction slightly smaller --- exactly four images per species, or 13.3\% of the pool --- while CIFAR-100 divides exactly. The published test sets are never touched, so test results remain comparable with the numbers reported in the literature. Table~\ref{tab:protocol_splits} reports the resulting partition.

Two facts in this table matter more than the totals. The first is the amount of supervision per leaf class, which differs by more than an order of magnitude: a CIFAR-100 fine class is trained on 450 images, whereas a CUB species is trained on 25 or 26 and an Aircraft variant on 33 or 34. Any observation about fine-grained behaviour on CUB and Aircraft is therefore made in a low-data regime that CIFAR-100 does not share. The second is the split composition: CIFAR-100 devotes 75\% of its images to training and 8\% to validation, whereas the official FGVC-Aircraft partition devotes only one third to training and a full third to validation. Aircraft consequently trains on the smallest pool of the three while offering the largest model-selection set, and the reliability of the checkpoint-selection procedure is not comparable across datasets.

Sample balance itself needs no further discussion. Leaf-class counts are uniform or near-uniform in every training split (exactly 450 per class on CIFAR-100, 33 or 34 per variant on Aircraft, 25 or 26 per species on CUB), and the only visible dispersion is the CUB test split, which ranges from 11 to 30 images per species. Class imbalance is therefore not a confound in these experiments; imbalance of the \emph{hierarchy}, examined next, is a different matter.

The three trees have visibly different shapes despite having the same depth.

FGVC-Aircraft is the most evenly distributed at the top --- its largest manufacturer covers 22 of 100 variants --- but it is the most degenerate at the bottom, as the icicle makes visible in the many family rectangles whose width is identical to that of the single variant beneath them.

\subsubsection{How much of the fine decision the coarse level already makes}

Branching statistics can be summarized in a single quantity per transition: the uncertainty about the child class that remains once the parent is known. Measuring it in bits as the conditional entropy $H(\text{child}\mid\text{parent})$, and comparing it against the $H(\text{child})$ that would remain without any hierarchy, gives a direct measure of how much information a correct coarse prediction supplies. Figure~\ref{fig:conditional_entropy} reports both.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/dataset/hierarchy_conditional_entropy.pdf}
\caption{Uncertainty about the child class of each transition, in bits. The full bar is the uncertainty without the hierarchy, $H(\text{child})$; the colored part is what remains once the parent is known, $H(\text{child}\mid\text{parent})$; the grey part above it, annotated with its percentage, is what the hierarchy removes. Values are structural, that is, computed from the taxonomy with a uniform distribution over classes.}
\label{fig:conditional_entropy}
\end{figure}

The three datasets separate clearly. On FGVC-Aircraft, identifying a variant among 100 costs $\log_2 100 = 6.64$ bits without the hierarchy, but knowing the family leaves only $0.78$ bits --- the equivalent of fewer than two equally likely variants --- an 88\% reduction that is the direct consequence of the 54 single-variant families. On CUB-200-2011 the same transition leaves $3.10$ of $7.64$ bits, roughly nine equally likely species, a reduction of 59\%; the order-to-family transition is the weakest of all six, at 48\%, because one order dominates. CIFAR-100 sits in between and is the most uniform across its two transitions, at 66\% and 65\%.

Two qualifications are necessary. First, these values are \emph{structural}: they are computed with a uniform distribution over classes and therefore describe the label tree alone. The analysis also computes the empirical version, weighted by training-image counts. The two agree to within $0.13$ bits on every transition and are identical on CIFAR-100, where each fine class has exactly 450 training images, which confirms that the effect measured here is a property of the taxonomy rather than of sample imbalance. Second, and more importantly, this is a statement about the label space, not a prediction: it says how much a correct coarse prediction constrains the leaf, not how easily a model recovers that constraint, and not whether a method that enforces the constraint will classify better. It does, however, delimit what a hierarchy-exploiting method can gain on each benchmark, and it makes the three datasets genuinely complementary --- one where the coarse level nearly determines the leaf (Aircraft), one where it is dominated by a single branch (CUB), and one where the two transitions are balanced (CIFAR-100).

\subsubsection{Image geometry and its interaction with the model presets}

Figure~\ref{fig:image_geometry} shows the native resolution of the training images. The three datasets occupy three separate regions: CIFAR-100 collapses into a single point at $32\times32$, CUB-200-2011 has a median of $500\times375$ pixels, with no sampled image exceeding 500 pixels on its long side, and FGVC-Aircraft has a median of $1024\times699$ pixels, roughly four times the area of a CUB image and seven hundred times that of a CIFAR-100 image.

\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/dataset/native_image_geometry.pdf}

The small open square marks the $224\times224$ input used by most model presets. This matters because the presets do not consume these images at a single resolution. CIFAR-100 is used natively at $32\times32$ by every model. CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft are resized to $224\times224$ by the H-CAST, Hier-COS, and LH-DNN presets, to $448\times448$ by the HRN preset, and to $64\times64$ by the HT-CapsNet preset. An Aircraft image is therefore downsampled by a factor of roughly 14 in area for a $224$-pixel model, 3.6 for HRN, and 175 for HT-CapsNet. These values follow each method's own configuration rather than a common normalization, so input resolution is a confound that is \emph{not} controlled in this comparison: any difference in fine-grained accuracy between HRN and the $224$-pixel models on CUB and Aircraft is partly attributable to it. The choice is stated explicitly among the threats to validity rather than silently normalized away, since imposing a single resolution would depart from the settings under which each baseline is designed to operate.

\subsubsection{Summary and threats to validity}

The analysis supports four statements that are used when interpreting the experimental results.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Supervision differs by an order of magnitude per class.} CIFAR-100 provides 450 training images per leaf class against 25--34 for CUB and Aircraft, so the two fine-grained benchmarks are low-data regimes and CIFAR-100 is not.
  \item \textbf{The trees are unbalanced in different ways.} CUB is dominated by one order covering 66.5\% of the species; Aircraft is nearly degenerate at its lower transition, with 77\% single-variant families; CIFAR-100 is perfectly regular below and deliberately irregular above.
  \item \textbf{The hierarchy carries very different amounts of information.} A correct parent removes 88\% of the leaf uncertainty on Aircraft, 59\% on CUB, and 65\% on CIFAR-100. The datasets therefore probe hierarchical methods in complementary rather than redundant conditions.
  \item \textbf{Two experimental factors are not controlled.} Split composition is inherited (Aircraft validates on a third of its data, CIFAR-100 on 8\%), and input resolution is inherited from each method's published configuration (64, 224, or 448 pixels on the fine-grained datasets).
\end{itemize}

Finally, the descriptive nature of this analysis bounds what it can support. The statistics above are computed on the taxonomies of Section~\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}, two of which are not published with their datasets; they would change under a different, equally defensible hierarchy over the same images. Native image geometry describes the source data and not the tensors seen by a model after resizing, cropping, and augmentation. And none of these quantities predicts performance: they describe the conditions under which the methods are compared, and they are used in the results sections below only to explain results, never to anticipate them.

\section{Metrics}
\label{ssec:inference_metrics}

\Cref{subsec:metrics} reviewed the measures available for hierarchical
classification and grouped them into three families: set- and pair-based
measures built on the ancestors shared by prediction and ground truth
(hierarchical precision, recall and F-measure, the unified framework of
Kosmopoulos et al.~\cite{kosmopoulos2015evaluation}, the hierarchical confusion
matrix~\cite{riehl2023hierarchical}); distance-based measures, which weigh an
error by how far it falls within the tree (tree-induced error, Mistake Severity,
average hierarchical distance, HOPS); and consistency measures, which ask
whether the predicted path is structurally admissible at all (TICE, full-path
accuracy). That survey is deliberately broader than what any single study
reports. This section fixes the subset used throughout the experiments and
states why it is that subset.

\paragraph{Selection criteria.} Three considerations determine the choice, and
they select the same four quantities.

\emph{Coverage.} Four questions must be answered separately for a comparison
between mechanisms to be informative: how often each level is correct on its own
(per-level accuracy); how often the entire path is correct (FPA); whether the
path is admissible under the taxonomy (TICE); and how severe the mistakes are
when they occur (AHD). None of the four is implied by the others. The per-level
accuracies are marginals and therefore say nothing about how errors co-occur
across levels, so they constrain neither FPA nor TICE. FPA is blind to the
structure of the errors on the samples it counts as wrong: two models with equal
FPA can differ in whether their mistakes remain inside the correct coarse class,
which is what AHD measures. And no accuracy measure recognises an impossible
path as impossible --- a prediction that is wrong at level $2$ and right at
level $3$ is credited once by $\operatorname{Acc}_3$ and penalised nowhere for
being unrealisable, which is what TICE measures. Four numbers are therefore the
smallest set that leaves no one of the four questions unanswered.

\emph{Comparability with the baselines.} Each of the four is already in use in
the line of work the baselines belong to, so no claim made below rests on an
instrument devised for this thesis, and none rests on a metric introduced
together with the method it evaluates. Per-level accuracy is the common currency
of every multi-head method of \cref{sec:baselines}; full-path accuracy is the
metric under which H-CAST reports its results~\cite{park2024visually}; TICE is
due to Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023consistency}, and LH-DNN~\cite{fiaschi2024informed}
and HD-CapsNet~\cite{noor2024consistency} report consistency measures of their
own; the average hierarchical distance is the severity measure of the
computer-vision literature, and the reference against which Hier-COS positions
HOPS~\cite{sani2025hiercos}.

\emph{Uniform computability.} All four are functions of the decoded path and the
ground-truth path alone. They require neither calibrated probabilities nor a
ranking over classes, and are therefore defined identically for a family whose
native readout is a per-level score and for one whose readout is a subspace norm
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}). Since the object of the study is precisely a
comparison across such families, a metric that presupposes one readout would
prejudge the question.

\paragraph{Measures deliberately not reported.} The remaining measures of
\cref{subsec:metrics} are omitted for reasons specific to this protocol, not
because they are inferior in general.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Set-based measures (hP, hR, hF) and the hierarchical confusion
    matrix.} These are designed for settings this protocol does not exhibit:
    multi-label predictions, partial paths, DAG-shaped taxonomies, non-mandatory
    leaf prediction. Here every prediction assigns exactly one node per level of
    a tree of fixed depth $L$. Under that restriction they add nothing to AHD.
    Let $m$ be the length of the longest matching prefix of $\hat y$ and $y$. If
    $\hat y$ is consistent, both ancestor sets have cardinality $L$ and their
    intersection is exactly that prefix, so
    $\mathrm{hP}=\mathrm{hR}=\mathrm{hF}=m/L$, whence
    $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{hF}]=1-\mathrm{AHD}/L$, and the tree-induced error is
    $2(L-m)$. If $\hat y$ is inconsistent the identity fails, but in the
    unhelpful direction: the intersection may contain a node whose ancestor was
    missed, so a set-based score rewards exactly the configuration that TICE
    exists to expose. The pair (TICE, AHD) is kept for that reason.
    \item \textbf{Mistake Severity.} MS averages the same quantity as AHD but
    over the misclassified samples only, so the set it averages over changes with
    the model. It is consequently not comparable across models of different
    accuracy, and can move for the sole reason that a model has stopped making
    the shallow errors that were pulling its average down. AHD averages over all
    samples and is free of this coupling.
    \item \textbf{HOPS.} As discussed in \cref{subsec:hiercos}, HOPS is normalised,
    order-sensitive and a genuine improvement on AHD as a single-number summary.
    That is also why it is not used here: it folds accuracy and severity into one
    score, whereas the claims of this thesis concern the trade-off between path
    validity and fine-level discrimination, which a single scalar hides by
    construction. It further requires a full ranking of the leaves per sample
    rather than a decoded path, and among the baselines it is reported by
    Hier-COS alone.
\end{itemize}

The metrics below are functions of a decoded path, so they are defined relative to
a chosen cell of the grid of \S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}. Unless a table states
otherwise, results are reported under each family's native readout, and always
under both decoders as separate quantities; rows from other cells of the grid are
labelled as such. Writing $\hat y=(\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_L)$ for the decoded path and
$y$ for the ground-truth path:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Per-level accuracy.} $\operatorname{Acc}_l
    =\Pr[\hat y_l=y_l]$, reported for each level. \emph{Higher is better.}
    \item \textbf{Weighted average precision (wAP).} The mean of the per-level
    accuracies weighted by the number of classes at each level,
    \[
    \mathrm{wAP}=\frac{\sum_{l}C_l\,\operatorname{Acc}_l}{\sum_l C_l}.
    \]
    The weights are the class counts $C_l$ and are unrelated to the objective's
    level weights $w_l$ of \S\ref{ssec:problem_objective}; since $C_L\gg C_1$ in
    all datasets used here, wAP is dominated by the finest level. It is not one
    of the four axes above but a compact summary of the per-level accuracies,
    reported so that tables admit a single-column comparison; no claim is made on
    its basis that the per-level accuracies do not already support.
    \emph{Higher is better.}
    \item \textbf{Full-path accuracy (FPA)}~\cite{park2024visually}. The
    exact-match rate over the whole path,
    $\mathrm{FPA}=\Pr[\hat y_l=y_l\ \text{for all }l]$. It is the strictest
    of the accuracy measures and the primary quantity for checkpoint selection.
    \emph{Higher is better.}
    \item \textbf{Average hierarchical distance (AHD).} The expected number of
    levels separating the prediction from the ground truth, measured as the depth
    below the deepest level at which the prediction still agrees with the label,
    \[
    \mathrm{AHD}=\mathbb{E}\Big[\,L-\max\{m: \hat y_l=y_l\ \text{for all } l\le m\}\Big],
    \]
    that is, $L$ minus the length of the longest matching prefix, with the
    maximum taken to be $0$ when $\hat y_1\neq y_1$. It equals $0$ on a fully
    correct path and $L$ when even the coarsest level is wrong, and it
    distinguishes a near miss inside the correct coarse class from a mistake at the
    top of the taxonomy. On a consistent path this is exactly the height of the
    lowest common ancestor of $\hat y_L$ and $y_L$, which is the definition given
    in \cref{subsec:metrics}; the prefix form is used here because under
    independent decoding $\hat y$ need not correspond to a node of the tree at
    all. \emph{Lower is better.}
    \item \textbf{Tree-induced consistency error (TICE).} The fraction of samples
    whose decoded path is \emph{not} consistent,
    $\mathrm{TICE}=1-\Pr[\pi_l(\hat y_{l+1})=\hat y_l\ \text{for all }l]$, in the
    sense of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023consistency}. It is a property of the
    prediction alone and does not involve the labels.
    \emph{Lower is better.}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Diagnostic quantities.} The five metrics above are what the tables
report. Several further quantities are computed at every evaluation and used in
the analysis chapters to explain \emph{why} a headline number moved; they are
listed here so as to be defined before they are used, and they never substitute
for the metrics above. All three taxonomies of \S\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}
have $L=3$; writing level $2$ for the middle level, level $3$ for the finest, and
$p_3$ for the model's fine-level probability vector:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conditional fine accuracy.} The fine-level accuracy restricted
    to the samples whose middle level was predicted correctly,
    $\Pr[\hat y_3=y_3\mid \hat y_2=y_2]$, reported together with its support
    $\Pr[\hat y_2=y_2]$ and separately per decoder. It separates a change in
    fine-level accuracy caused by better fine discrimination from one inherited
    from the level above --- a distinction that top-down decoding makes
    unavoidable, since there the feasible set is determined by $\hat y_2$.
    \item \textbf{Ground-truth parent mass.} The probability the model assigns to
    the sibling group of the true fine class,
    $\sum_{c:\pi_2(c)=y_2}p_3[c]$, evaluated on the raw and on the effective
    logits. It measures whether a mechanism concentrates mass on the correct
    branch, which is precisely the quantity that \cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}
    identifies as movable by an affine hierarchy correction.
    \item \textbf{Rank of the ground-truth class among its siblings.} The
    $1$-based rank of $y_3$ within $\{c:\pi_2(c)=y_2\}$ by fine-level score,
    averaged over samples. By the same corollary this quantity is invariant under
    the HCC correction, which makes it a useful control: a change in it cannot be
    attributed to the projection.
    \item \textbf{Projection deltas.} For runs with an active HCC cascade, the
    mean $\ell_1$ displacement between raw and effective fine-level scores, the
    fraction of samples whose fine argmax the projection flips, and the signed
    change at the ground-truth class. These quantify how much work the constraint
    is doing, as opposed to whether it is nominally enabled.
\end{itemize}

One caveat applies to the consistency axis. By
\cref{prop:topdown_consistency}, $\mathrm{TICE}$ is identically zero under
top-down decoding, so the informative quantity is
$\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{independent}}$, which measures whether a model produces
coherent scores on its own rather than whether the decoder was made to enforce
coherence. A comparison of TICE across methods is therefore meaningful only in
the independent column; the top-down column is reported as a check that the
decoder behaves as specified.

\section{Implementation fidelity and deviations from the published models}
\label{sec:fidelity}

The data and the metrics are now fixed. What is not yet fixed is the object being
measured. \Cref{ch:methods} described the mechanisms and the inference rules as
though they applied to a single, uniform substrate; they do not. They apply to
five model families, and those families are the one component of the apparatus
that this work did not define.

The five families compared in this thesis were published as five separate
codebases, in two deep learning frameworks, with five evaluation protocols, five
label conventions and, in one case, no released code at all. Comparing them
required reimplementing or porting all of them into the single framework of
\cref{ch:methods}. That decision is what makes the comparisons of this chapter
meaningful, and it is also the largest threat to their external validity, because
every reimplementation is an opportunity to change a result without intending to.

This section therefore records what was changed and why. It is written to be
falsifiable rather than reassuring: each deviation is classified, and deviations
that place a configuration outside what the original work evaluated are marked as
such so that no claim below can silently rest on one. It is placed here, before
the experimental design, because the design's central caveat --- that a
difference between two family rows compares implementations rather than
mechanisms (\S\ref{ssec:design_threats}) --- is only as credible as the record
that follows.

Throughout, \emph{aligned} means that the local implementation reproduces the
method-defining behaviour of the source --- its architecture, objective and
inference rule --- as verified by the tests of \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_verification}.
It does not mean that a local run reproduces a published number. It cannot: the
protocol below deliberately differs from several of the originals, and framework,
library, hardware and seed differences prevent numerical identity even where the
equations agree.

\subsection{The unified protocol, and what it costs}
\label{ssec:fidelity_protocol}

A set of protocol decisions is applied identically to every family. They are
listed together because they are the reason cross-family numbers can be placed in
one table at all, and because each of them silently breaks compatibility with at
least one of the original repositories.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item One coarse-to-fine label convention with a fixed number of levels, and
    one canonical label space reused across training, validation and test.
    \item A deterministic, stratified train/validation partition with a fixed
    split seed, so that all families see the same partition of the same data.
    \item Every validation and test sample is evaluated; incomplete final batches
    are not dropped.
    \item Checkpoints are selected on validation data only, never on test.
    \item Two checkpoints are kept per run, one per decoding mode, ranked by the
    lexicographic criterion of \S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}.
    \item Shared metrics, logging artefacts, mixed-precision gating and strict
    resume checks.
\end{enumerate}

Items 3 and 4 are corrections rather than conveniences. At least one reference
implementation drops incomplete evaluation batches, and at least one can select
or report on test data; reproducing either would import an optimistic bias into
this thesis, so neither is reproduced. The cost is stated plainly: a run in this
framework is not a reproduction of a published run, and no number in this
chapter should be read against a published table. The comparisons
that this thesis makes are within-framework comparisons, in which every arm pays
the same protocol cost.

\subsection{A classification of the deviations}
\label{ssec:fidelity_classes}

Every difference from a reference implementation falls into one of five classes,
and the class determines what may be claimed from the resulting run.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Framework adaptation.} Plumbing required to run the model in
    this framework: data loading, logging, checkpointing, the protocol of
    \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_protocol}. Method-neutral by construction.
    \item \textbf{Port.} A translation between frameworks or library versions
    which is intended to preserve semantics exactly. Ports are the class most
    likely to introduce silent error, and are therefore the class most heavily
    covered by the tests of \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_verification}.
    \item \textbf{Verified correction.} A place where the reference
    implementation is internally inconsistent, disagrees with its own paper, or
    relies on an artefact of its local environment, and a decision had to be
    taken. Each such decision is recorded with the evidence that motivated it.
    \item \textbf{Local extension.} Behaviour that is not in the original at all:
    HCC, lexicographic mode, and the transplant of the LH-projection onto
    Hier-COS. These are contributions of this thesis and must never be attributed
    to the authors of the host family.
    \item \textbf{Unsupported extrapolation.} A dataset, hierarchy or input
    geometry the original work never evaluated. Results from these settings are
    reported separately and are not used to support any claim about the published
    method.
\end{itemize}

The extrapolations are named here in one place, since they recur in the results:
HRN on CIFAR-100, Hier-COS on CUB-200-2011 and on a three-level rather than
five-level CIFAR hierarchy, HT-CapsNet on FGVC-Aircraft and on this framework's
CUB taxonomy, and LH-DNN on CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft.

\subsection{Family by family}
\label{ssec:fidelity_families}

\paragraph{H-CAST.} The CAST and H-CAST internals are vendored unmodified, and
the local wrapper only reorders their level outputs into the repository-wide
coarse-to-fine convention. The segmentation machinery and the level-wise and
global-KL loss paths follow the upstream design. One verified correction was
required: the upstream Aircraft helper derives its hierarchy from a local
CSV with a partly hard-coded taxonomy, which is not reconstructible outside that
repository; the local adapter instead joins the official parallel
manufacturer/family/variant annotations distributed with the dataset and
validates the resulting $30/70/100$ taxonomy. Remaining divergences are shape and
forward-contract guards required by current \texttt{timm} and PyTorch versions,
each covered by a test. HCC and lexicographic mode are local extensions on this
family.

\paragraph{LH-DNN.} Reconstructed from \cite{fiaschi2024informed}. The
reconstruction follows the paper on the CIFAR-100 $8\to20\to100$ hierarchy, the
large topology (four pairs of $3\times3$ convolutions, four $2\times2$ pooling
stages, a shared $512$-unit rectified layer and linear level heads), the
detached branch projections whose forward value is unchanged --- the behaviour
established in general in \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_forward} --- the advantage
topology in which coarser predictions are propagated as detached baselines, an
unweighted sum of per-level cross-entropies, and the paper's short CIFAR schedule
with its learning-rate switch. Two extrapolations are recorded: the paper
evaluates CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 and Fashion-MNIST rather than the fine-grained
datasets used here, and on those datasets the final feature map is reduced by a
deterministic average pool, which preserves the paper's pre-head geometry at
$224$~px and avoids an accidental parameter explosion in the dense head. The
transforms and regularisation used on those datasets are local choices and are
not reported as paper settings.

\paragraph{HT-CapsNet.} The most invasive case, since the source is a
TensorFlow/Keras implementation and the local one is a PyTorch port. Ported with
intent to preserve semantics: primary capsules, per-level secondary capsules, the
hierarchical skip input, routing by agreement, taxonomy masking, cross-capsule
attention, the margin loss, and the dynamic level weighting. Three decisions
required evidence beyond the paper. First, the released code and the paper give
different formulas for the dynamic level weights; the port follows the released
callback, on the grounds that it is what produced the published numbers, and the
weights are stored as checkpointed buffers so that each batch uses the weights
computed after the preceding one, as the Keras callback does. Second, the
attention block retains Keras semantics, in which heads have independent key and
value widths, so the projection width does not depend on the capsule dimension;
this is verified against an explicit reference implementation in the tests.
Third, the constructor defaults of the source are internally inconsistent, and
the local taxonomy temperature follows the saved run arguments of the released
experiments instead. Two framework adaptations are deliberate failures rather
than fallbacks: a missing taxonomy and missing pretrained weights both raise,
because both would otherwise silently degrade the model --- into an ordinary
capsule network and into a randomly initialised network labelled as pretrained.
The local CUB taxonomy is the unified one of this framework rather than the
paper's, and FGVC-Aircraft is an extrapolation, the paper having reported a
different fine-grained dataset.

\paragraph{HRN.} Aligned on the ResNet-50 trunk, the three residual feature
branches, the sigmoid tree scores with a separate leaf cross-entropy head, the
residual fusion path, and the evaluation rule in which the two coarser levels are
read through sigmoids and the finest through a softmax. The fine-grained presets
retain the source resize/crop geometry, normalisation, SGD schedule and reduced
trunk learning rate. Divergences are protocol: complete evaluation batches and
validation-only selection, as in \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_protocol}; the
partial-label branch of the original is out of scope; mixup and cutmix are
disabled for parity with the source objective. CIFAR-100 is an extrapolation
which retains this framework's native $32$~px preprocessing rather than the
source's fine-grained input geometry, chosen for cross-family comparability and
labelled accordingly.

\paragraph{Hier-COS.} Aligned on the taxonomy-node coordinate system and its
subspaces, the random orthonormal fixed frame, the objective combining the global
path distribution with the level-wise regulariser, the backbone families, the
dataset-specific preprocessing, and --- importantly for
\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout} --- the inference rule, which ranks raw subspace
projection norms directly and applies no softmax before prediction. One source
ambiguity is recorded rather than resolved: the released launch scripts pass
different feature-space flags for different datasets, so the fine-grained recipe
cannot be described as an unambiguous instance of the published method; the local
preset is a consistent adaptation and is labelled as such. The decomposed
objectives of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos} are local extensions introduced because
the published objective exposes no per-level term, and the three-level CIFAR
hierarchy is a deliberate departure from the upstream five-level protocol, taken
so that all families in this thesis share one hierarchy depth.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{ssec:fidelity_verification}

Fidelity claims are asserted by an automated test suite rather than by
inspection, and the same suite verifies the propositions of \cref{ch:methods}. It
covers, on the port side, the capsule squashing and margin-loss equations against
their source form, the attention projection shapes against an explicit reference,
the taxonomy masking pattern, the HRN tree objective, its parameter grouping and
its schedule, and the preset configurations of both fidelity families. On the
mechanism side it verifies that the disabled HCC path is bitwise identical to
the corresponding unconstrained run, that the projected
scores satisfy the hierarchy constraints, that the correction shifts each sibling
group by a single constant and leaves the coarsest level anchored ---
\cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift} --- that top-down decoding agrees with a
naive reference loop, and that the subspace-norm readout reproduces native
Hier-COS inference, as claimed in \S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}.

What such tests establish is agreement at the level of equations, shapes and
invariants. They cannot establish that a trained local model reproduces a
published trained model, and no claim in this thesis depends on their doing so.

\section{Experimental design and protocol}
\label{sec:results_design}

The apparatus is now complete. \Cref{ch:methods} derived the four
mechanisms and established, in \S\ref{sec:methodology_axis}, that they form a
five-way single choice rather than a factorial; \S\ref{sec:datasets} and
\S\ref{ssec:inference_metrics} have fixed the data the comparison runs on and the
quantities it is scored with. What remains is to state which questions the
admissible configurations answer, and under what rules an observed difference is
allowed to count as an answer.

This section states them in the order in which they constrain each other. It
first asks what the comparison \emph{runs on}, since two of the four mechanisms
are only definable on particular substrates and the cost of moving to those
substrates has to be charged somewhere (\S\ref{ssec:design_substrate}); it then
states the questions the chapter was built to ask, about whether the locus at
which priority is imposed makes a difference (\S\ref{ssec:design_locus}) and
about the one mechanism that imposes no priority at all
(\S\ref{ssec:design_objective}); it isolates the arms that exist solely to falsify an attribution rather than to
support one (\S\ref{ssec:design_controls}); it records the variables held fixed
and the threats that survive holding them (\S\ref{ssec:design_threats}); it fixes
the reporting rules applied without exception (\S\ref{ssec:design_protocol}); and
it closes with the inventory of what was actually run
(\S\ref{sec:results_campaign}).

One asymmetry between the two groups of questions is worth stating in advance.
The substrate questions are, in part, preconditions: per-level normalisation and
an identity frame are what make gradient-space ordering and the LH-projection
definable at all, so their cost is the entry price of the mechanisms that follow
and is reported as such, separately, before any mechanism is applied. The locus
questions are the thesis proper. Reading a substrate result as though it were a
mechanism result --- or absorbing it silently into a mechanism-versus-baseline
delta --- is the specific error this ordering exists to prevent.

\subsection{Substrate questions: what the comparison runs on}
\label{ssec:design_substrate}

Before the mechanisms can be compared, the platform they are compared on has to
be characterised. Three questions do that. The first two carry a second reading
as enabling conditions, which is what makes them part of the argument rather
than preliminaries.

\paragraph{Is Hier-COS's benefit the fixed frame, or the inference rule?} Hier-COS
bundles three things that are usually credited as one: a frozen orthonormal frame
over taxonomy nodes, a subspace-norm readout (\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}), and
a loss with a level regulariser. The published contribution narrative attaches to
the frame. What tests it is a three-point ladder --- a dense random orthonormal
frame, a per-level block-diagonal one, and the identity --- with the node-block
layout, the readout and everything else held fixed. All three rungs are
orthonormal, so the ladder varies exactly two things: cross-level coordinate
mixing at the first step, and the within-level rotation at the second. If the
identity matches the dense frame, the frame is not doing the work and the honest
claim becomes one about the output parametrisation and the readout; if it does
not, the middle rung says which of the two removals is responsible. The post-hoc
grid sharpens this: the subspace-norm readout applies to any family
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}), so the readout half of the bundle can be
tested on models never trained with a frame at all, without retraining
anything.

The second reading: the block-diagonal rung is a \emph{precondition} of the
LH-projection, not an optional extra. Under a dense frame the coordinates of any
level are mixtures of the outputs of all heads, so the map from the shared
representation to a level's scores involves the finer heads' weights as well and
conditions~1--2 of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting} fail
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(i)). This ladder is therefore a prerequisite for
the locus comparison of \S\ref{ssec:design_locus} rather than a standalone
ablation.

\paragraph{Where must the softmax live?} Normalising across all taxonomy nodes at
once, against normalising within each level, looks like a loss detail and is in
fact the structural gate for part of the design (\S\ref{ssec:design_space}). It
carries two questions. As a standalone ablation: does per-level normalisation cost
accuracy relative to global, at matched frame and weights? As an enabling
condition: the two scopes are not gates for the same thing. Decomposing the
published objective into $L$ terms while keeping the global normaliser induces
identical gradients (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(ii)), and that decomposition is
all lexicographic mode requires, which is why the Hier-COS lexicographic arm is
run at the global scope and pays no entry price here. Scoping the normaliser to
the level is a strictly further step, and it is the LH-projection that needs it:
only then is each level term a function of its own branch alone, so that
filtering what that branch reads determines the whole of its signal. Whatever
per-level normalisation costs is therefore the \emph{entry price} of the
LH-projection specifically, and must be reported as such rather than absorbed
silently into a mechanism-versus-baseline delta. This is the reason the baseline
is run in both scopes: a mechanism arm at per-level softmax compared against a
baseline at global softmax confounds the mechanism with the normaliser.

\paragraph{Does gradient-space ordering need a learnable transform to act on?}
Progressively removing the learnable transformation between backbone and frame
--- from the full residual transform, to a batch-norm-plus-linear reduction, to
none at all --- is a mechanism question, not a capacity question. Gradient
projection acts on whatever parameters lie in the projected path. If the benefit
shrinks as the transform shrinks, the effect is localised to a learned
re-embedding; if it survives with no transform in the optimiser path, the
mechanism is acting on the backbone itself, and the claim generalises to any
architecture --- which is what the cross-family extension of
Table~\ref{tab:framework_support} depends on.

\subsection{Locus questions: does it matter where priority is imposed?}
\label{ssec:design_locus}

These are the questions the chapter was built to ask, and they map one-to-one onto
the points of the computation at which the four mechanisms act, as classified in
\S\ref{ssec:design_mechanisms}.

\paragraph{Does gradient-space ordering work, and is the \emph{ordering} what
matters?} The first half is a comparison against the matched baseline. The second
half is the harder one and requires the composition modes of
\S\ref{ssec:lex_modes} to be treated as an experimental axis rather than an
implementation choice: \texttt{coarse\_first} is the hypothesis and
\texttt{fine\_first} inverts the priority while applying exactly the same
quantity of gradient surgery. Without the second, an observed improvement is
equally compatible with ``projecting gradients at all helps''. A third question
concerns the scale rather than the direction of the level contributions: the
projection fixes which directions a lower-priority objective may act in but not
how large its surviving contribution is (\S\ref{ssec:problem_lr}), so the
taxonomy-derived scalings of Table~\ref{tab:eta_branching} are treated as an
axis of their own.

\paragraph{One principle, two implementations: LH-projection against lexicographic
mode.} By \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_forward} these are not complementary methods but the
same idea realised twice. The in-graph formulation is elegant and cheap --- the
constraint is expressed once in the forward pass and the backward pass inherits it
--- but the guarantee it obtains is only what the graph gives: first-order, per
sample, at the branch point, and conditional on the activation. The explicit
formulation ignores how the graph is built, takes whatever gradients emerge and
orthogonalises them directly; it costs \(L\) reverse-mode derivative evaluations
and up to \(L\) simultaneous gradient copies, with no activation assumption.
Its protection is exact on two-level blocks but applies only to the assembled
higher-priority reference on a three-level block
(\S\ref{ssec:lex_guarantee}). The question is therefore not whether they stack --- they
cannot (\S\ref{ssec:design_axis}) --- but whether the cheap in-graph formulation
produces the same empirical hierarchy consistency, or whether its branch-point
restriction carries a measurable cost. The head-to-head runs at matched frame,
loss, weights and seeds, scored primarily on TICE and AHD against the shared
baseline. Two sub-variants of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos} then probe where the
in-graph guarantee is thinnest: the representation width, which sets how much
room condition~4 of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting} leaves above the reserved
subspace, and the advantage parameterisation, which changes what that subspace
is reserved against.

\paragraph{Output space against gradient space.} This is the contrast that keeps
the thesis honest about what ``lexicographic'' means. HCC constrains outputs; the
other two constrain gradients. Per \S\ref{ssec:design_axis} the achievable cells
are exactly the four mechanism conditions of the single-choice axis, read
against the shared \emph{none} baseline that completes it, so this is a clean
between-arms comparison and the absence of combination arms is
itself the result. One sub-question refines it decisively. HCC is part of the
forward computation at inference as well as during training
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_interaction}), so it can be applied at evaluation alone,
at no training cost, to a checkpoint trained without it
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}). That separates a pure output correction from
whatever training under the constraint adds, and no claim about HCC should be
made before it is settled. The same section fixes which cells can register such
an effect: with signed node scores only independent decoding can change, while
magnitude and subspace-norm readouts may change under either decoder.

\subsection{Objective question: does supervising the ranked quantity help?}
\label{ssec:design_objective}

Direct subspace supervision is the one mechanism of \cref{ch:methods} that
imposes no ordering (Table~\ref{tab:framework_mechanisms}), so it does not
belong to the locus family above and is not a competitor to it in the same
sense. Its question is narrower and entirely internal to Hier-COS: the native
objective supervises the taxonomy-node coordinates while the decoder ranks the
subspace norms computed from them (\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}), and the
mechanism closes that gap by supervising the ranked quantity directly
(\S\ref{sec:methodology_subspace}). Does training on what is ranked improve what
is ranked?

Two things must be kept apart when the answer is read. First, the mechanism is
inactive at inference: the readout and decoders are unchanged, so any measured
difference is a difference in the parameters learned. Second, the target of
\eqref{eq:subspace_target} is reachable but its attainable margin shrinks with
depth --- $1.000$, $0.423$ and $0.184$ from coarse to fine for $L=3$
(\cref{prop:subspace_margin}) --- and the temperature $\tau$ is what rescales
those margins inside the softmax. $\tau$ is therefore an experimental axis of
the arm rather than a fixed constant, and an arm reported at a single $\tau$
identifies nothing about the mechanism. The arm carries the further restrictions
of \S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting}: hard path labels, so label smoothing, mixup
and cutmix are disabled, and no combination with any of the other three
mechanisms.

\subsection{Controls and falsification arms}
\label{ssec:design_controls}

Two configurations exist solely to falsify an attribution, and are recorded here
so that they are not mistaken for hypotheses.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inverted priority.} The \texttt{fine\_first} composition mode of
    \S\ref{ssec:lex_modes} applies exactly the same quantity of gradient surgery
    with the ordering reversed, on the same blocks, which are fixed by the
    architecture and do not change with the priority. It is the only control the
    operator admits, since \S\ref{ssec:lex_modes} defines exactly two composition
    orders; consequently it alone carries the burden of separating ``coarse-first
    ordering helps'' from ``projecting at all helps'', and the central claim of
    \S\ref{ssec:design_locus} is not identified without it.
    \item \textbf{Coarse-heavy weighting.} If the reported benefit of
    gradient-space ordering is consistency rather than accuracy, then reweighting
    the scalarised objective towards the coarse levels is a much cheaper way to buy
    the same thing. A coarse-heavy weight vector tests exactly that. If it
    reproduces the consistency gain without any projection, the claim about
    gradient-space ordering weakens sharply --- it would mean the mechanism is one
    way to move along an accuracy--consistency trade-off and simple reweighting is
    another.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Controlled variables and threats to validity}
\label{ssec:design_threats}

\paragraph{Level weights.} The weighting $w_l$ of \S\ref{ssec:problem_objective}
is the most consequential controlled variable, but its interaction with
lexicographic mode is family-specific (\S\ref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}).
H-CAST and HT-CapsNet expose unweighted level tensors, so their lexicographic
arms use unit participation; this differs from any matched-family baseline with
dynamic weighting, notably the native HT-CapsNet baseline. Hier-COS instead
embeds its level coefficients in the classification terms supplied to the
projection, while HRN defines no analogous coefficient.
A pair that differs in this respect changes two variables at once and is labelled
accordingly; matched-objective and matched-weight controls are used wherever
available. Two questions must be kept apart: whether the weight choice is a
large effect, and whether the \emph{ranking of methods} is weight-dependent. The
second is substantive --- a method that wins only at one weight setting has not
been shown to win.

\paragraph{Training budget.} The native HT-CapsNet baseline follows its
paper-aligned 200-epoch schedule. Explicit lexicographic training is materially
more expensive because it evaluates and combines the three level gradients, so
the HT-CapsNet lexicographic arm is limited to 100 epochs. Its comparison with
the native baseline is therefore not compute- or epoch-matched: a difference in
final performance cannot be attributed to the gradient intervention alone.

\paragraph{Backbone and pretraining.} A from-scratch, CIFAR-native wide residual
backbone and an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 bound every other conclusion. This
axis is not a contribution; it exists to establish whether the frame, weighting
and mechanism effects are conditional on the feature extractor. If they invert
between the two, every conclusion in the thesis is conditional in the same way.

\paragraph{Dataset scope and local extrapolations.} The three datasets differ in
how much the hierarchy actually constrains the fine label: a coarse semantic
grouping over small images, a fine-grained set with shallow semantic spread, and a
genuinely hierarchical manufacturer--family--variant taxonomy. That is the property
every mechanism here depends on, so a method that works on one and not another is
reporting a requirement, not a failure. Separately, several settings used here
depart from the configurations the original works evaluated --- among them HRN on
CIFAR-100, Hier-COS on CUB, and this repository's construction of the CIFAR
hierarchy. Results from those settings are reported separately from paper-aligned
ones and are not used to support claims about the published methods.

\paragraph{Cross-family comparisons are between implementations.} The families
compared here do not differ only in their hierarchical mechanism. They differ in
backbone, optimiser, augmentation, input geometry and, in the case of HRN, in the
activation through which each level is read
(\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_families}). Each family's settings were kept close to its
own published recipe rather than forced into a common one, on the grounds that a
family evaluated outside its own hyperparameter regime is not the published
method; the consequence is that a difference between two families confounds the
mechanism with the recipe. Cross-family rows are therefore read as evidence about
implementations, and every mechanism claim in this thesis is instead identified
\emph{within} a family, against that family's own matched baseline. This is the
reason the head-to-head of \S\ref{ssec:design_locus} is run inside a single
family.

\paragraph{Hyperparameter effort is not uniform.} Hyperparameter search was
carried out for one family on one dataset, by an automated study over its
learning rate, weighting and transform settings; the remaining families and
datasets use published or preset values. The asymmetry favours the searched cell
and is disclosed wherever a table includes it. No mechanism arm was tuned
separately from the baseline it is compared against, so the within-family
comparisons of \S\ref{ssec:design_locus} are not affected; cross-family rows are.

\paragraph{Statistical treatment and multiplicity.} Comparisons in this thesis
are descriptive. Where more than one seed is available, the mean and sample
standard deviation are reported with the seed count, and differences are computed
per seed and then averaged wherever seeds are matched, which removes the
seed-to-seed variance common to both arms; no significance test is applied, and
no claim rests on a difference smaller than the observed seed spread. A second
form of multiplicity arises from the inference grid: a checkpoint can be scored
under eight cells, and selecting the most favourable one after the fact would
convert evaluation freedom into an apparent effect. The rule stated in
\S\ref{ssec:design_protocol} therefore fixes the native cell as the reported
result and treats the remaining cells as diagnostics.

\paragraph{Coverage.} The design above describes the space the apparatus admits,
not a claim that every cell of it has been populated. What an unpopulated cell
costs, and which claims it leaves undecided, is the subject of
\cref{sec:results_coverage}.

\subsection{Protocol and reporting rules}
\label{ssec:design_protocol}

The following are applied without exception, and several of them exist to prevent
a specific error that the apparatus of \cref{ch:methods} makes possible.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One mechanism at a time.} The main experiments vary a single
    mechanism against a matched baseline, so that an effect can be attributed to a
    locus. By \S\ref{ssec:design_axis} the mechanism axis is a single choice among
    five conditions, no combination arm exists, and none is planned.
    \item \textbf{Both decoders are always reported}, for every configuration,
    each from its own checkpoint (\S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}): a top-down row
    from the top-down-selected checkpoint, an independent row from the
    independent-selected one. Mixing them would confound the decoder comparison
    with a checkpoint comparison. A decoder is never treated as a property of a
    method.
    \item \textbf{The structural preconditions of the LH-projection are not
    ablations of it.} The identity frame and the decomposed level objective are
    what make the mechanism definable on Hier-COS at all
    (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}). Their comparison against the published
    configuration measures the cost of adapting the baseline, and is reported
    before, and separately from, any comparison involving the projection itself.
    \item \textbf{Mechanism activation is verified from diagnostics, not from
    configuration.} That a run was configured with a mechanism does not establish
    that the mechanism was active. HCC activation is read from the logged
    \texttt{proj\_constraint\_alpha}, which records that the cascade was in force
    for the batches of the reported epoch; claims about gradient-space ordering
    are grounded in the logged pre- and post-projection cosines, the removed
    norms, and the firing rate of the degeneracy guard of
    \S\ref{ssec:lex_projection}, on which \cref{prop:lex_firstorder} is
    conditional. A directory name or a preset name records the intent and not the
    outcome.
    \item \textbf{Metric directionality is fixed.} Full-path accuracy, weighted
    average precision and per-level accuracy are higher-is-better; average
    hierarchical distance and tree-induced consistency error are lower-is-better.
    Differences between percentages are reported in percentage points.
    \item \textbf{The reported cell is the native one.} A result is quoted under
    the family's own readout, with the remaining cells of the grid of
    \S\ref{ssec:inference_grid} used as diagnostics and labelled as such. The
    most favourable cell is never promoted to the headline number after the fact.
    \item \textbf{Seeds are reported, never assumed.} Seed coverage is uneven
    across the arms, so every aggregate carries its seed count, and the mean is
    accompanied by the sample standard deviation whenever more than one seed
    exists. A single-seed arm is labelled as such and supports no claim of stable
    superiority; the seed count of every configuration is stated in the
    inventory of \S\ref{sec:results_campaign} and repeated with each table.
    Epoch-level curves are filtered for runs whose logs terminate early before
    being averaged, since a truncated log is not a truncated run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Organisation of the experimental campaign}
\label{sec:results_campaign}

The remaining sections report what the protocol of \S\ref{ssec:design_protocol}
produced, in the order in which the results become readable rather than in the
order in which the mechanisms were introduced.

\Cref{sec:results_baselines} establishes the reference point: every family run
under the unified protocol with no mechanism active. Nothing in the chapter is
interpretable without it, since every mechanism claim is identified within a
family against that family's own matched baseline
(\S\ref{ssec:design_threats}). \Cref{sec:results_inference} then scores those
same frozen checkpoints under the eight cells of the inference grid
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}). It comes second because it retrains nothing and
because its outcome fixes how every subsequent number is to be read: it
separates what a readout rule contributes from what a representation
contributes, and it establishes what the HCC constraint is worth as a pure
output correction before any run trained with it is discussed.
\Cref{sec:results_hcc}, \cref{sec:results_lex} and \cref{sec:results_subspace}
report three of the four mechanisms against their matched baselines, each with
the controls that make an observed effect attributable. The fourth, the
LH-projection, is reported in \cref{sec:results_ablations} instead, because it
is definable only once the structural preconditions of
\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos} have been measured in their own right, and reading
its arm before that cost is known would charge the preconditions to the
mechanism. \Cref{sec:results_mechanisms} collects the mechanism axis of
\S\ref{ssec:design_axis} --- one choice among five conditions, the shared
\emph{none} baseline included --- into a single comparison.
\Cref{sec:results_ablations} reports the substrate and locus ablations, which
are run only where the preceding sections give a reason to run them. Finally,
\cref{sec:results_coverage} states which cells of the design are unpopulated and
which claims are consequently unidentified, and \cref{sec:results_summary}
collects what the chapter supports.

\section{Inference: readout, transform and decoder}
\label{sec:results_inference}

Everything in this section is computed on the frozen baseline checkpoints of
\cref{sec:results_baselines}. No parameter is trained, no loss is constructed,

(\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}). The section therefore measures what can be
obtained from an unchanged representation by changing only the rule that reads
it, and it is placed before the mechanism sections because that quantity is the
correct subtrahend for every later claim: a mechanism that improves a metric by
no more than its own readout does has not been shown to change the
representation.

\subsection{Is the ordering what matters?}

\subsection{Relative gradient scale}
\label{ssec:results_lex_scaling}

The projection fixes the directions in which a lower-priority objective may act
but not the size of its surviving contribution, and the projected contributions
are still summed (\S\ref{ssec:problem_lr}). Relative gradient scale is therefore
a degree of freedom of the mechanism that leaves the priority order untouched,
and reusing the weights tuned for the scalarised baseline is a choice rather
than a neutral default: those weights balance an unprojected sum, whereas the
quantities actually added here are the projected residuals.

The arms are the four assignments of Table~\ref{tab:eta_branching} against
uniform scale, all fixed before training and derived from the class counts
alone. Cumulative branching scales a level by $C_l^\beta$, so the exponent
controls how strongly the finest level dominates; marginal branching scales by
the average fan-out of the transition into that level and, unlike the
cumulative rule, need not increase with depth --- on FGVC-Aircraft it places its
largest scale on the middle level. The two rules therefore separate on that
dataset and nearly coincide on the other two, which is what makes it the
informative one for this ablation.

\subsection{Where the in-graph guarantee is thin}
\label{ssec:results_lhproj_variants}

Two of the three qualifications of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_limits} can be probed
directly. The reserved subspace is built from
$A^{(l)}[b]=W_{<l}\operatorname{diag}(\rho'(k_b))$, so it is rebuilt per sample
from the PReLU derivative at that sample's own pre-activation
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(iii)); how much is removed along a reserved
direction is $1-\varepsilon/(\sigma_i^2+\varepsilon)$ and degrades as the
singular values of $A^{(l)}[b]$ approach zero, so the logged singular spectrum
is reported beside the metrics rather than assumed benign. The representation
width sets how much room exists above the reserved subspace: condition~4 of
\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting} requires $D>\sum_{l<L}C_l$, and at $D$ close to that
bound the complement the finest level is left to act in is nearly empty. The
advantage parameterisation of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(iii), in which each
head predicts a correction to its detached parent logit rather than an absolute
score, is carried as the third row because it changes what the reserved subspace
is reserved \emph{against}.
