\chapter{Experiments}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter evaluates the methods of \cref{ch:methods} on CIFAR-100,
CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft. Five model families use common dataset
partitions, label hierarchies and metrics. Each intervention is compared within
a family against an unmodified model trained under the same settings.
Cross-family results are also reported, but they compare complete architectures
and training recipes rather than isolating one hierarchical mechanism.

After describing the datasets, metrics, implementations and comparison protocol,
the chapter establishes the baselines and first evaluates alternative inference
rules on those baselines alone. The trained interventions are then considered
according to where they act: HCC on the outputs, direct subspace supervision on
the objective, and lexicographic ordering on the gradients. Ablations of the
Hier-COS frame, normaliser and level weighting quantify the adaptations required
by the LH-projection and test the sensitivity of gradient ordering to structural
configuration and relative gradient scale. Once all mechanisms have been
reported, the inference analysis is repeated on the explicit lexicographic and
LH-projection checkpoints. Within the LH-projection analysis, a CIFAR-100
backbone-capacity ladder tests whether the in-graph mechanism depends on the
default feature extractor. The final sections compare the computational cost of
the two gradient-priority mechanisms and summarise the supported conclusions.

\subsection{Dataset selection}
\label{sec:dataset_selection}

No dataset is shared by all five original evaluation suites. CIFAR-100,
CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft each appear in three of them, and together cover
at least one original dataset for every family. This overlap applies only at the
dataset level; hierarchies and protocols may still differ. The unsupported
family--dataset pairs are identified as extrapolations in
\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_families}.

Within that coverage constraint, the selection combines complementary data and hierarchy
regimes, rather than treating any one of the three as uniquely standard. CIFAR-100 is a generic,
low-resolution recognition dataset with substantially more training images per leaf class; CUB and
FGVC-Aircraft are higher-resolution fine-grained datasets with much less leaf-level supervision.
Their hierarchies also have different provenance: CIFAR-100 supplies two levels and receives an
external top-level grouping, CUB supplies only the species labels and requires a reconstructed
taxonomy, and FGVC-Aircraft distributes annotations for all three levels used here.
Table~\ref{tab:datasets_info} summarises their characteristics.

\subsubsection{CIFAR-100}
CIFAR-100 appears in the original evaluation suites of LH-DNN, HT-CapsNet and Hier-COS
\cite{fiaschi2024informed,noor2025taxonomy,sani2025hiercos}; this is the same three-of-five coverage
as each of the other selected datasets. Its distinct role is instead the data regime. The official
dataset contains 100 fine classes grouped into 20 superclasses, with $32\times32$ images and 500
published training images per fine class \cite{krizhevsky2009learning}. After the validation split
used in this work, 450 images per leaf class remain, compared with 25--26 for a CUB species and
33--34 for an Aircraft variant. Only the fine and superclass levels are official; the eight-class
top level used by the common three-level protocol is the external B-CNN grouping described in
\S\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}.

\subsubsection{CUB-200-2011}
CUB-200-2011 likewise appears in the original evaluation suites of three families: H-CAST, HRN and
HT-CapsNet \cite{park2024visually,chen2022label,noor2025taxonomy}. It contributes a fine-grained
biological domain with 200 leaf classes and only 25--26 training images per species under the common
protocol. The dataset itself publishes species labels but no order or family labels
\cite{wah2011caltech}; consequently, the upper levels must be inherited from a reconstruction.
This makes CUB useful for testing a reconstructed natural taxonomy, but it also prevents unqualified
comparison of published scores: H-CAST and HRN use a 13/38/200 tree, whereas HT-CapsNet reports a
39/123/200 tree. Section~\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction} documents the tree adopted here and the
remaining uncertainty about its provenance.

\subsubsection{FGVC-Aircraft}
FGVC-Aircraft is the third dataset with three-family prior coverage, having been evaluated by H-CAST,
HRN and Hier-COS \cite{park2024visually,chen2022label,sani2025hiercos}. Like CUB, it is a
fine-grained, low-data benchmark; unlike CUB, its manufacturer, family and variant annotations and
its train/validation/test partition are distributed with the dataset \cite{maji2013fine}. It
therefore provides a second fine-grained visual domain while avoiding reliance on an externally
reconstructed label taxonomy. The official split leaves only 33--34 training images per variant,
and the resulting 30/70/100 tree has a markedly different branching structure from both CUB and
CIFAR-100, as quantified in \S\ref{sec:dataset_analysis}. The LH-DNN and HT-CapsNet rows on this
dataset are extrapolations, not original evaluation settings.


\subsection{Hierarchy construction}
\label{sec:hierarchy_construction}

The three label trees have different origins, and only one is published as part
of its dataset. Because the models are trained against these trees and AHD and
TICE are defined on them, their provenance is part of the experimental protocol.
The following sections document the source of every level, the choices made in
this work and the remaining uncertainties.

Three properties are enforced identically across the three datasets, so that the comparison between
models is not confounded by the shape of the label space. First, all hierarchies have exactly three
levels, which is the depth required by H-CAST and HRN and the depth at which all considered methods
can be run without modification. Second, every class has exactly one parent at every level, so the
taxonomy is a tree and not a directed acyclic graph. Third, the label space --- the mapping from raw
dataset identifiers to the contiguous class indices seen by the models --- is constructed once from
the training split and reused unchanged by validation and test, which prevents split-local
relabelling and guarantees that a class index denotes the same concept in every split.

\subsubsection{CIFAR-100 hierarchy}

CIFAR-100 \cite{krizhevsky2009learning} publishes two of the three levels. The 100 fine labels are
the leaves, and the 20 official superclasses --- which the dataset distributes as
\texttt{coarse\_label\_names} --- form the middle level. This mapping is regular by construction:
each superclass contains exactly five fine classes.

The dataset provides no third level. The top level used here is the grouping of the 20 superclasses
into 8 coarse classes introduced by the B-CNN reference implementation \cite{zhu2017bcnn}, which is
adopted so that the CIFAR-100 hierarchy is three levels deep and therefore directly comparable with
the other two benchmarks.\footnote{The grouping is taken from the published B-CNN source,
\url{https://github.com/zhuxinqimac/B-CNN/blob/main/CIFAR_100_keras_vgg16_hierarchy_dynamic.py}, and
is reproduced verbatim in the data adapter of this work.} The resulting eight groups are:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*), itemsep=0pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item aquatic mammals, fish;
  \item flowers, fruit and vegetables, trees;
  \item food containers, household electrical devices, household furniture;
  \item insects, non-insect invertebrates;
  \item large carnivores, large omnivores and herbivores, medium mammals, reptiles, small mammals;
  \item large man-made outdoor things, large natural outdoor scenes;
  \item people;
  \item vehicles~1, vehicles~2.
\end{enumerate}

Two consequences follow and are carried through the rest of this work. The top level is an
\emph{external, manually defined} semantic grouping, not a property of CIFAR-100: results obtained
on it describe this particular taxonomy and should not be read as results on ``the'' CIFAR-100
hierarchy. Furthermore, the grouping is deliberately uneven --- the mammal-and-reptile group
collects five superclasses while the \emph{people} group collects one --- so CIFAR-100 combines a
perfectly regular lower transition with a visibly irregular upper one, a combination that turns out
to be useful when interpreting the analysis in \S\ref{sec:dataset_analysis}.

\subsubsection{CUB-200-2011 hierarchy}

CUB-200-2011 \cite{wah2011caltech} publishes 200 species and their images, but no higher taxonomic
level. The hierarchy used here is the 13 orders / 38 families / 200 species tree retained from the
H-CAST codebase \cite{park2024visually}, stored in this work as an explicit species-to-(order,
family) table so that it is versioned together with the experiments rather than reconstructed at
load time.

The codebase does not name the levels, so they were identified by inspecting the class names
attached to each group; they are orders, families and species. The same 13/38/200
order--family--species tree is reported independently by HRN \cite{chen2022label}, which
reconstructs the CUB taxonomy following the procedure of Chang et al.\ \cite{chang2021flamingo}. Two
independent reconstructions agreeing is the strongest corroboration available for a tree that the
dataset does not publish, and it is why this one was preferred to the alternatives in the
literature.

HT-CapsNet \cite{noor2025taxonomy} instead uses a 39/123/200 hierarchy, which could not be traced to
a verifiable source. It is taken from a second paper whose label files are distributed through links
that no longer resolve, and that paper in turn takes it from a third. In the third source the
39/123/200 split appears to correspond to family/genus/species, but two things do not match: that
work states a 36/113/200 hierarchy, and it reports roughly 80\% accuracy, comparable to H-CAST,
whereas HT-CapsNet reports 20--30\% on the metrics it shares with the other works. Since the chain
cannot be closed, this tree was not adopted.

A preliminary LLM-assisted audit also flagged possible discrepancies in the
H-CAST hierarchy. Because this audit did not use an authoritative taxonomy, it
does not constitute verification. The adopted hierarchy still requires a
systematic check of every order, family and species against an authoritative
biological database before it can be treated as fully validated.

\subsubsection{FGVC-Aircraft hierarchy}

FGVC-Aircraft \cite{maji2013fine} is the only one of the three benchmarks that publishes a complete
hierarchy, and it is used here without any external input. The official download distributes the
class lists (\texttt{manufacturers.txt}, \texttt{families.txt}, \texttt{variants.txt}) and, for
every split, three \emph{parallel} per-image annotation files that label the same images at the
manufacturer, family, and variant levels. The tree is therefore not published as a set of
parent--child edges: it has to be recovered by joining the three annotation files on the image
identifier.

This work performs that join and, in doing so, verifies the assumptions it relies on: the three
annotation files of a split are required to cover exactly the same image identifiers; every variant
must be observed with a single family and every family with a single manufacturer across the entire
dataset, so that the recovered relation is a function; and the resulting parent maps must cover
every published class. Violations raise an error rather than being silently repaired. The resulting
hierarchy has 30 manufacturers, 70 families, and 100 variants, matching the official class lists.

The official annotation also defines a fourth, finer \emph{model} level, which is not used: three
levels are kept for comparability with CIFAR-100 and CUB-200-2011, and the variant level is the one
on which the published FGVC-Aircraft results of HRN, H-CAST, and Hier-COS are reported.

\subsubsection{Taxonomy provenance}

The three trees consequently have different epistemic status, and this asymmetry is a threat to the
external validity of any cross-dataset claim made in this work. The FGVC-Aircraft hierarchy is
official and fully verifiable from the distributed annotations. The CUB-200-2011 hierarchy is
inherited from H-CAST and corroborated by an independent reconstruction in HRN, but it has not yet
been validated against an authoritative biological taxonomy. The CIFAR-100 top level is externally
imposed and is one grouping among many possible ones. Results are therefore reported for the
explicit taxonomies described above, and no claim is made that they transfer to a different
hierarchy defined over the same images.


\subsection{Dataset analysis}
\label{sec:dataset_analysis}

This section characterises the inputs to the unified protocol: data splits,
supervision per class, tree imbalance and the uncertainty about a fine label that
remains after its parent is known. The analysis notebook computes every value
from the training data adapters, using the same split sizes, label indices and
hierarchy edges as the models. These statistics describe the data and label
space, not model behaviour.

\subsubsection{Splits and class coverage}

Table~\ref{tab:datasets_info} reports the datasets as they are distributed. The protocol departs
from those published partitions in one respect only, and it does so because model selection requires
a validation set that is disjoint from the test set. FGVC-Aircraft ships an official
train/validation/test partition, which is used unchanged. CIFAR-100 and CUB-200-2011 publish only a
train/test partition, so a validation split is carved deterministically out of the published
training pool: the split is stratified at the leaf level, uses seed $0$, and reserves a nominal
10\% of the CIFAR-100 training pool and 15\% of the CUB training pool. Per-class rounding makes the
realised CUB fraction slightly smaller --- exactly four images per species, or 13.3\% of the pool
--- while CIFAR-100 divides exactly. The published test sets are never touched, so test results
remain comparable with the numbers reported in the literature. Table~\ref{tab:protocol_splits}
reports the resulting partition.

Supervision per leaf class differs by more than an order of magnitude: 450
images for a CIFAR-100 fine class, compared with 25 or 26 for a CUB species and
33 or 34 for an Aircraft variant. Fine-grained behaviour on CUB and Aircraft is
therefore observed in a low-data regime not shared by CIFAR-100. The split
composition also differs. CIFAR-100 assigns 75\% of its images to training and
8\% to validation, whereas the official FGVC-Aircraft split assigns one third to
each partition. Aircraft consequently has the smallest training pool and the
largest model-selection set, so checkpoint selection is not equally reliable
across datasets.

Sample balance needs no further discussion: leaf-class counts are uniform or near-uniform in every
training split, and the only visible dispersion is the CUB test split, at 11 to 30 images per
species. Class imbalance is not a confound in these experiments; imbalance of the \emph{hierarchy},
examined next, is a different matter.

\subsubsection{Taxonomy structure}

Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy_icicle} shows the three taxonomies drawn to scale: the three bands are the
three levels, every rectangle is a class, its width is the fraction of leaf classes in its subtree,
and children are drawn beneath their parent. The three trees have visibly different shapes despite
having the same depth.

CIFAR-100 is regular at the bottom and irregular at the top: every superclass has exactly five fine
classes, but the eight imposed coarse classes cover between 5 and 25 fine classes. CUB-200-2011 is
dominated by a single top-level class, which covers 133 of the 200 species (66.5\%); the two largest
orders together cover 78\%. FGVC-Aircraft is the most evenly distributed at the top --- its largest
manufacturer covers 22 of 100 variants --- but it is the most degenerate at the bottom, as the
icicle makes visible in the many family rectangles whose width is identical to that of the single
variant beneath them.

family. CIFAR-100 has almost none, by construction.

\subsubsection{Coarse-level information}

Branching statistics can be summarised in a single quantity per transition: the uncertainty about
the child class that remains once the parent is known. Measuring it in bits as the conditional
entropy $H(\text{child}\mid\text{parent})$, and comparing it against the $H(\text{child})$ that
would remain without any hierarchy, gives a direct measure of how much information a correct coarse
prediction supplies. Figure~\ref{fig:conditional_entropy} reports both.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/dataset/hierarchy_conditional_entropy.pdf}
\caption{Uncertainty about the child class of each transition, in bits. The full bar is the
uncertainty without the hierarchy, $H(\text{child})$; the coloured part is what remains once the
parent is known, $H(\text{child}\mid\text{parent})$; the grey part above it, annotated with its
percentage, is what the hierarchy removes. Values are structural, that is, computed from the
taxonomy with a uniform distribution over classes.}
\label{fig:conditional_entropy}
\end{figure}

The three datasets separate clearly. On FGVC-Aircraft, identifying a variant among 100 costs $\log_2
100 = 6.64$ bits without the hierarchy, but knowing the family leaves only $0.78$ bits --- the
equivalent of fewer than two equally likely variants --- an 88\% reduction that is the direct
consequence of the 54 single-variant families. On CUB-200-2011 the same transition leaves $3.10$ of
$7.64$ bits, roughly nine equally likely species, a reduction of 59\%; the order-to-family
transition is the weakest of all six, at 48\%, because one order dominates. CIFAR-100 sits in
between and is the most uniform across its two transitions, at 66\% and 65\%.

These values are \emph{structural}: they assume a uniform distribution over
classes and describe the label tree alone. Weighting by training-image counts
changes every transition by at most $0.13$ bits and leaves CIFAR-100 unchanged,
so sample imbalance has little effect on the reported values. The statistic also
describes the label space rather than model performance. It measures how much a
correct parent constrains its child, not whether a model recovers that constraint
or benefits from its enforcement. On this axis, Aircraft is close to determined
by the parent, CUB is dominated by one branch, and CIFAR-100 has two similarly
balanced transitions.

\subsubsection{Image resolution and model presets}

Figure~\ref{fig:image_geometry} shows the native resolution of the training images. The three
datasets occupy three separate regions: CIFAR-100 collapses into a single point at $32\times32$,
CUB-200-2011 has a median of $500\times375$ pixels, with no sampled image exceeding 500 pixels on
its long side, and FGVC-Aircraft has a median of $1024\times699$ pixels, roughly four times the area
of a CUB image and seven hundred times that of a CIFAR-100 image.

This matters because the presets do not consume these images at a single resolution. CIFAR-100 is
used natively at $32\times32$ by every model, but CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft are resized to
$224\times224$ by the H-CAST, Hier-COS and LH-DNN presets, to $448\times448$ by HRN, and to
$64\times64$ by HT-CapsNet. An Aircraft image is thus reduced in area by a factor of roughly 14 for
a $224$-pixel model, 3.6 for HRN, and 175 for HT-CapsNet. Each figure follows the method's own
published configuration rather than a common normalisation, so input resolution is a confound that
is \emph{not} controlled here: part of any fine-grained accuracy gap between HRN and the $224$-pixel
models on CUB and Aircraft is attributable to it. Imposing a single resolution would have removed
the confound at the price of evaluating each baseline outside the regime it was designed for, so the
confound is declared instead.

\subsubsection{Summary and threats to validity}

Four properties of the experimental setting are relevant to the results.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Supervision differs by an order of magnitude per class.} CIFAR-100 provides 450
      training images per leaf class against 25--34 for CUB and Aircraft, so the two fine-grained
      benchmarks are low-data regimes and CIFAR-100 is not.
  \item \textbf{The trees are unbalanced in different ways.} CUB is dominated by one order covering
      66.5\% of the species; Aircraft is nearly degenerate at its lower transition, with 77\%
      single-variant families; CIFAR-100 is perfectly regular below and deliberately irregular
      above.
  \item \textbf{The hierarchy carries very different amounts of information.} A correct parent
      removes 88\% of the leaf uncertainty on Aircraft, 59\% on CUB, and 65\% on CIFAR-100. The
      datasets therefore probe hierarchical methods in complementary rather than redundant
      conditions.
  \item \textbf{Two experimental factors are not controlled.} Split composition is inherited
      (Aircraft validates on a third of its data, CIFAR-100 on 8\%), and input resolution is
      inherited from each method's published configuration (64, 224, or 448 pixels on the
      fine-grained datasets).
\end{itemize}

The statistics depend on the taxonomies of
\S\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}, two of which are not published with their
datasets and would change under a different defensible tree. Native image
geometry also describes source files rather than tensors after resizing,
cropping and augmentation. These observations characterise the comparison
conditions; they do not predict an experimental result.

\section{Metrics}
\label{ssec:inference_metrics}

\Cref{subsec:metrics} defines and analyses the available measures. The
experiments retain the five quantities below because together they separate
marginal correctness, whole-path correctness, mistake severity and structural
validity, while remaining computable from every baseline's decoded output.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{0.23\textwidth} p{0.54\textwidth} p{0.12\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Measure} & \textbf{Question answered} & \textbf{Direction} \\
\midrule
$\operatorname{Acc}_l$ & Is level $l$ correct on its own? & Higher \\
wAP & What is the class-count-weighted summary of level accuracies? & Higher \\
FPA & Is the complete predicted path correct? & Higher \\
AHD & How far below the last correct ancestor does the error occur? & Lower \\
TICE & Does the predicted tuple violate a parent--child edge? & Lower \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Their definitions are given in
\cref{eq:metric_accuracy,eq:metric_wap,eq:metric_fpa,eq:metric_ahd,eq:metric_tice}.
FPA is the primary checkpoint-selection quantity. In wAP, the class counts
$C_l$ are unrelated to the objective weights $w_l$ of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_objective}; wAP is reported only as a compact summary and
never in place of the per-level accuracies. The alternatives are not repeated
here: in this fixed-depth, single-path protocol hP, hR and hF are algebraically
redundant on valid paths, while the additional generality of the HCM is not
exercised; MS is conditioned on error; and HOPS requires a full leaf ranking and
combines accuracy with severity, whereas this study analyses those effects
separately.

All five measures are evaluated relative to a chosen cell of the inference grid
of \S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}. Unless a table states otherwise, results use each
family's native readout and report independent and top-down decoding separately.
One protocol-specific convention is required for AHD. A tuple produced by
independent decoding need not correspond to a leaf path, so let

\[
m_i=\max\{m:\hat y_{il}=y_{il}\ \text{for every }l\leq m\},
\qquad
\mathrm{AHD}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(L-m_i),
\]

with $m_i=0$ when the coarsest prediction is wrong. For a valid prediction this
is exactly the LCA-height definition in \cref{eq:metric_ahd}; for an invalid
tuple it is a conservative extension based on the longest correct prefix. This
is the only metric definition specialised by the experimental protocol.

\paragraph{Diagnostic quantities.} The tables report the five metrics above.
Additional quantities are computed at each evaluation to interpret changes in
those metrics. They are defined here but do not replace the primary measures.
All three taxonomies of \S\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}
have $L=3$; writing level $2$ for the middle level, level $3$ for the finest, and
$p_3$ for the model's fine-level probability vector:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conditional fine accuracy.} The fine-level accuracy restricted
    to the samples whose middle level was predicted correctly,
    $\Pr[\hat y_3=y_3\mid \hat y_2=y_2]$, reported together with its support
    $\Pr[\hat y_2=y_2]$ and separately per decoder. It separates a change in
    fine-level accuracy caused by better fine discrimination from one inherited
    from the level above --- a distinction that top-down decoding makes
    unavoidable, since there the feasible set is determined by $\hat y_2$.
    \item \textbf{Ground-truth parent mass.} The probability the model assigns to
    the sibling group of the true fine class,
    $\sum_{c:\pi_2(c)=y_2}p_3[c]$, evaluated on the raw and on the effective
    logits. It measures whether a mechanism concentrates mass on the correct
    branch, which is precisely the quantity that \cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}
    identifies as movable by an affine hierarchy correction.
    \item \textbf{Rank of the ground-truth class among its siblings.} The
    $1$-based rank of $y_3$ within $\{c:\pi_2(c)=y_2\}$ by fine-level score,
    averaged over samples. By the same corollary this quantity is invariant under
    the HCC correction, which makes it a useful control: a change in it cannot be
    attributed to the projection.
    \item \textbf{Projection deltas.} For runs with an active HCC cascade, the
    mean $\ell_1$ displacement between raw and effective fine-level scores, the
    fraction of samples whose fine argmax the projection flips, and the signed
    change at the ground-truth class. These quantify how much work the constraint
    is doing, as opposed to whether it is nominally enabled.
\end{itemize}

One caveat applies to the consistency axis. By
\cref{prop:topdown_consistency}, $\mathrm{TICE}$ is identically zero under
top-down decoding, so the informative quantity is
$\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{independent}}$, which measures whether a model produces
an independently decoded valid path on its own rather than whether the decoder
was made to enforce one. A comparison of TICE across methods is therefore
meaningful only in the independent column; the top-down column is reported as a
check that the decoder behaves as specified.

\subsection{Common evaluation protocol}
\label{ssec:fidelity_protocol}

All families use the same coarse-to-fine label convention, dataset partitions
and label indices. Validation and test include every sample, and checkpoints are
selected on validation data only. A run retains the mode-specific candidates
defined in \S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}, but every paired post-hoc inference
comparison uses one fixed, independently selected checkpoint. Metrics, logging,
mixed-precision checks and resume validation are also shared.

Every configuration is trained three times, from the seeds $0$, $1$ and $2$. The
seed varies only the randomness internal to training --- network initialisation
and the other stochastic elements of the training loop --- and never the data:
the validation splits carved out of the CIFAR-100 and CUB-200-2011 training
pools (\S\ref{sec:dataset_analysis}) are built once with seed $0$ and reused by
every run, so that all arms and all seeds see identical partitions. The same
three seeds are used for every arm, which is what \emph{matched seeds} denotes
in the remainder of the chapter: where two arms share them, the difference
between them is computed seed by seed and averaged only afterwards, so that the
comparison is paired rather than made between independent draws. Three
repetitions are enough to report a sample standard deviation with each mean and
not enough to support a significance test; none is applied
(\S\ref{ssec:design_threats}).

These choices correct two behaviours present in some reference codebases:
dropping incomplete evaluation batches and selecting results on test data. They
also mean that the experiments below are controlled comparisons inside this
framework, not exact reproductions of the published runs.

\subsection{Model adaptations}
\label{ssec:fidelity_classes}
\label{ssec:fidelity_families}

Three kinds of difference matter when interpreting the results. A
\emph{protocol adaptation} applies the common rules above without changing the
method-defining computation. A \emph{local extension}, such as HCC or
lexicographic mode, is a contribution of this thesis applied to a baseline. An
\emph{unsupported extrapolation} evaluates a family on a dataset or hierarchy
not covered by its source; such rows describe the local implementation and are
not evidence about the published method.

\paragraph{H-CAST.} The CAST and H-CAST internals, segmentation path and losses
are retained. The wrapper only converts outputs to the common coarse-to-fine
order. For FGVC-Aircraft, the local hierarchy is built from the official
manufacturer, family and variant annotations because the upstream helper relies
on a repository-local, partly hard-coded taxonomy. Compatibility guards for
current PyTorch and \texttt{timm} versions are covered by tests. HCC and
lexicographic mode are local extensions.

\paragraph{LH-DNN.} The reconstruction follows the reported
$8\to20\to100$ CIFAR hierarchy, large convolutional topology, detached branch
projections, advantage connections, unweighted level losses and 15-epoch
schedule. CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft are extrapolations. On their larger
$224$-pixel inputs, deterministic average pooling preserves the paper's
pre-head geometry and prevents the dense head from growing with image area.

\paragraph{HT-CapsNet.} The TensorFlow/Keras source was ported to PyTorch while
retaining its capsule stages, routing, taxonomy mask, attention, margin loss and
dynamic level weighting. Except for the capsule readout described below, where
the paper and code differ the port follows the released training code. In
particular, it uses
$1-\mathrm{acc}_l^{(t)}\omega_l^{\mathrm{init}}$ inside the dynamic-weight
callback, rather than the paper's
$(1-\mathrm{acc}_l^{(t)})\omega_l^{\mathrm{init}}$; the two versions are derived
and contrasted in \cref{subsec:htcapsnet}. It also retains the saved
taxonomy-temperature settings. The attention implementation is tested against
the Keras projection semantics.

The post-attention capsule readout is a deliberate protocol correction. The
paper and released implementation apply layer normalisation to the sum of the
routed capsule and its attention residual. This is incompatible with the
subsequent readout: class evidence is the Euclidean length of each capsule, and
the margin loss compares that length with $m^+=0.9$ and $m^-=0.1$. At the
default layer-normalisation parameters, a $d$-dimensional capsule has
$\lVert\operatorname{LN}(x)\rVert_2\simeq\sqrt{d}$ independently of its input
scale. For the released capsule dimensions $64$, $32$ and $16$, the initial
lengths are therefore approximately $8$, $5.66$ and $4$: they exceed both
margins and are almost constant across classes. The normalisation consequently
removes the magnitude that the capsule objective is designed to interpret as
presence evidence.

All HT-CapsNet experiments in this work instead apply the standard capsule
squash function to the same residual sum,
\[
\operatorname{squash}(x)
=\frac{\lVert x\rVert_2^2}{1+\lVert x\rVert_2^2}
  \frac{x}{\lVert x\rVert_2},
\qquad
0\leq\lVert\operatorname{squash}(x)\rVert_2<1.
\]
This substitution preserves the attention and routing computations while
restoring the bounded length semantics assumed by the margin loss. In a local
memorisation diagnostic on the same 16 CIFAR-100 images for 60 updates, the
released LayerNorm readout ended with total loss $362.15$ and accuracies
$1.00/0.06/0.00$, whereas changing only this readout to squash ended with loss
$0.082$ and accuracies $1.00/1.00/1.00$ in coarse-to-fine order. This diagnostic
isolates the failure mode but is not treated as benchmark evidence. Because the
substitution changes a computation specified by both the paper and its source,
the resulting HT-CapsNet rows evaluate a corrected local adaptation rather than
an exact reproduction. The unified CUB hierarchy also differs from the paper's
hierarchy, and FGVC-Aircraft is an extrapolation.

\paragraph{HRN.} The local model retains the ResNet-50 trunk, residual branches,
tree scores, leaf classifier, residual fusion and native sigmoid/softmax readout.
Fine-grained preprocessing and optimisation follow the source. The common
protocol changes evaluation and checkpoint selection; partial-label training is
out of scope, and mixup and cutmix are disabled. CIFAR-100 is an extrapolation
and uses its native $32$-pixel inputs. Lexicographic mode requires three
differentiable level objectives, which the local \texttt{level\_conditional}
mode supplies by the chain rule of \S\ref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}; because
its terms sum to the native objective, the arm is a local extension of the
paper-aligned model rather than a change of objective, and its baseline is the
native run.

\paragraph{Hier-COS.} The taxonomy-node coordinates, fixed orthonormal frame,
loss, backbones, preprocessing and subspace-norm inference are retained. The
released scripts use inconsistent feature-space flags across datasets, so the
local fine-grained preset is a documented adaptation. The decomposed objectives
of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos} are local extensions, and the three-level CIFAR
hierarchy departs from the source's five-level setting.

The unsupported family--dataset settings are therefore HRN on CIFAR-100;
Hier-COS on CUB-200-2011 and on the three-level CIFAR hierarchy; HT-CapsNet on
FGVC-Aircraft and on the unified CUB hierarchy; and LH-DNN on CUB-200-2011 and
FGVC-Aircraft.

\subsection{Verification and limitations}
\label{ssec:fidelity_verification}

Automated tests cover the ported capsule equations and attention shapes, the
taxonomy mask, the HRN objective and schedule, and the model presets. They also
verify that disabling HCC leaves the unconstrained computation unchanged, that
HCC satisfies its hierarchy constraints, that top-down decoding matches a
reference implementation, and that the subspace-norm readout reproduces native
Hier-COS inference.

These tests establish agreement of equations, shapes and invariants. They do not
establish that a trained local model is numerically identical to a published
checkpoint; the comparisons below do not rely on such an identity.

\section{Experimental protocol}

\subsection{Research questions}
\label{ssec:design_questions}

\paragraph{General question.} Does imposing a coarse-to-fine priority among the
levels of a taxonomy improve hierarchical classification, and if so, which
properties does it improve? More specifically, can an observed benefit be
attributed to the priority itself rather than to level weighting, changes to the
host model, or inference rules, and do mechanisms acting at different points of
the computation produce different accuracy--consistency trade-offs?

This general question is divided into the four research questions of
\cref{tab:design_overview}. The last column states the evidence required to
answer each question; it is part of the question's operational definition, not
merely a guide to the order of presentation.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\experimenttablesetup
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L{0.8cm}Y Y@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Research question} & \textbf{Identifying evidence} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Q1} & Relative to scalarised training with matched level weights, does
coarse-to-fine priority improve hierarchical classification, and is any change
found in predictive accuracy, full-path correctness, or structural consistency?
& Within-family, within-dataset comparison of each applicable priority arm with
its matched \emph{none} baseline, supported by mechanism-activation diagnostics \\
\textbf{Q2} & Is the ordering itself responsible for the effect, or can it be
reproduced by inverted priority or by coarse-heavy scalar weighting? & Matched
\texttt{coarse\_first}, \texttt{fine\_first}, ordinary baseline, and
coarse-heavy scalarisation arms \\
\textbf{Q3} & Do HCC, the LH-projection, and explicit lexicographic mode produce

do they arise during training or from inference-time processing? & Within-family
  deltas at the shared mechanism axis, the baseline and gradient-priority
  frozen-checkpoint inference grids,

\textbf{Q4} & Under which conditions does an effect persist: which model

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Research questions and the evidence required to identify them.}
\label{tab:design_overview}
\end{table}

The term \emph{priority mechanism} does not make the interventions
mathematically interchangeable. Explicit lexicographic mode and the
LH-projection order gradient contributions, although at different points and
granularities. HCC instead imposes an asymmetric output-space constraint; it has
lexicographic structure but does not solve the level objectives sequentially.
Direct subspace supervision is retained as a non-ordering objective-space
comparator and is therefore not counted among the three mechanisms in Q3. Its
role is to test whether changing the supervised hierarchical quantity can move
the same metrics without imposing a priority order.

Q1 is the primary effect question; Q2 tests its attribution. Q3 asks whether the
implementation locus matters and separates learned effects from inference-time
ones. Q4 establishes scope rather than universal generalisation. The questions
are therefore nested: a mechanism cannot support a positive answer to Q1 unless
its activity is verified, and any such answer is qualified by Q2--Q4.

All attribution claims use comparisons within the same family, dataset,
training recipe and, where available, matched seeds. Cross-family results answer
questions of implementation fidelity and help delimit Q4, but they do not
isolate a mechanism because the complete architectures and training recipes
differ. Reproducibility and comparability are treated as prerequisites in
\S\ref{sec:fidelity}, rather than as research questions in their own right.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection}
\label{ssec:design_checkpoints}

Independent and top-down decoding may rank training epochs differently. Every
run therefore retains one checkpoint per decoding mode, each selected on the
validation split using that mode's own lexicographic criterion. The formal rule
is defined in \S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}: FPA is compared first, ties are
resolved by lower TICE and then by higher wAP. Mode-specific checkpoints support
end-to-end pipeline reporting. Whenever the purpose is to isolate a readout,
decoder or post-hoc transform, however, all alternatives are evaluated on the
same independently selected checkpoint; otherwise the contrast would also
contain a checkpoint-selection effect.

\subsection{Identifying mechanism effects}
\label{ssec:design_locus}

The experiments compare four interventions with an unmodified baseline: HCC,
explicit lexicographic gradient ordering, the LH-projection, and direct
supervision of taxonomy-subspace scores. These conditions are mutually
exclusive (\S\ref{sec:methodology_axis}); the study evaluates one intervention
at a time rather than their combinations. Each trained intervention is compared
with its corresponding unmodified family at matched dataset and training recipe.

\paragraph{HCC: training effect or inference correction?} HCC changes the
forward computation both during training and at inference
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_interaction}). Applying it post hoc to an unconstrained
checkpoint measures the immediate correction; comparing that row with a model
trained under HCC measures what training with the constraint adds.

\paragraph{Explicit lexicographic ordering.} The proposed
\texttt{coarse\_first} mode is compared with the baseline and with
\texttt{fine\_first}, which reverses priority while using the same projection
operator (\S\ref{ssec:lex_modes}). Taxonomy-derived level scalings are varied
separately, because projection changes gradient directions but does not fix the
relative size of the surviving contributions (\S\ref{ssec:problem_lr}).

\paragraph{LH-projection against explicit ordering.} Both methods implement
coarse-to-fine protection, but at different points of the computation. The
LH-projection acts inside the graph at a branch point; explicit lexicographic
mode combines complete level gradients after backpropagation. They are compared
on Hier-COS with matched frame, loss, weights and seeds. Representation width
and advantage parameterisation are then varied to test the conditions on which
the in-graph formulation depends (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting}).

\paragraph{Comparison across intervention points.} HCC constrains outputs, the
two lexicographic methods constrain gradients, and direct subspace supervision
changes the objective. Each is compared with the shared \emph{none} condition.
The comparison concerns separate alternatives: their combinations are outside
the design defined in \S\ref{ssec:design_axis}.

\subsection{Direct supervision of subspace scores}
\label{ssec:design_objective}

The completed experiment is internal to Hier-COS. The objective is in principle
applicable to any family that exposes taxonomy-subspace scores, but Hier-COS is
the only baseline here whose native decoder already ranks those scores. Its
native loss supervises taxonomy-node coordinates, whereas inference ranks the
norms of taxonomy subspaces (\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}). Direct subspace
supervision therefore changes the trained quantity while leaving the readout
and decoder unchanged. Applying the same objective to a classifier-head family
would additionally replace its native node-score readout and would answer a
different question; that extension is not part of this campaign.

The attainable target margin decreases from coarse to fine
(\cref{prop:subspace_margin}), so the temperature $\tau$ is a sensitivity axis
of the mechanism rather than an innocuous constant. The completed campaign
evaluates $\tau=0.1$; the planned sweep was not completed and its consequence
for identification is stated in \S\ref{ssec:results_subspace_tau}. This arm uses
hard path labels, with label smoothing, mixup and cutmix disabled as required by
\S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting}.

\subsection{Hier-COS adaptations}
\label{ssec:design_substrate}

The LH-projection cannot be attached directly to published Hier-COS. It requires
independent level heads, an orthonormal frame that does not mix levels, and a
loss whose level terms depend only on their corresponding heads
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}). Two ablations measure the cost of introducing
those structural requirements; a third then fixes the relative level scale
before the LH-projection is compared with explicit lexicographic training.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Frame.} Dense, block-diagonal and identity frames are compared
    with the readout and node layout fixed. This separates cross-level mixing
    from within-level rotation and also establishes the cost of replacing the
    dense frame before the LH-projection is enabled.
    \item \textbf{Softmax scope.} Global and per-level normalisation are compared
    at the same frame and weights. Explicit lexicographic training only requires
    separate differentiable losses and can retain the global normaliser. The
    LH-projection requires per-level normalisation, because each loss must depend
    on its own branch alone.
    \item \textbf{Level weighting.} Uniform, Hier-COS leaf-heavy and
    class-count-derived weights are evaluated on the LH-projection arm at the
    selected frame and per-level normaliser. The unmodified and
    \texttt{coarse\_first} arms are held at one rule rather than re-run at each,
    because the weighting accumulates into the reserved subspace only under the
    in-graph locus (\S\ref{ssec:results_lhproj_weight_transfer}); the cost is
    that the weighting's main effect is not separated from a weight--mechanism
    interaction. Aircraft additionally carries the marginal-branching rule, whose
    largest coefficient is assigned to the middle level.
\end{enumerate}

The frame and softmax ablations first establish one LH-compatible structural
configuration per dataset. The weight ablation is then run on that fixed
configuration in three matched arms: unmodified, \texttt{coarse\_first} and the LH-projection.
This ordering prevents a frame or normaliser change from being folded into the
weight--mechanism interaction. The frame and softmax steps are reported before
the LH-projection (\cref{sec:results_substrate}); the weight cross is reported
with it (\cref{sec:results_lhprojection}), because the weighting cannot be fixed
on the lexicographic arm and transferred to the in-graph arm.

\subsection{Controls}
\label{ssec:design_controls}

Two controls test alternative explanations for a benefit attributed to
coarse-first gradient ordering.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inverted priority.} \texttt{fine\_first} applies the same
    projection operator as \texttt{coarse\_first}, but reverses the order. It
    separates the effect of coarse-first priority from the generic effect of
    modifying gradients.
    \item \textbf{Coarse-heavy weighting.} A scalar baseline with greater weight
    on coarse losses tests whether simple reweighting can reproduce any observed
    gain in hierarchical consistency. If it can, projection is not required to
    reach that accuracy--consistency trade-off.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{ssec:design_threats}

The following limitations determine how the result tables may be read.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HT-CapsNet capsule readout.} Every HT-CapsNet arm replaces the
    released post-attention LayerNorm with capsule squash, as justified in
    \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_classes}. This common correction keeps comparisons
    between local HT-CapsNet arms matched, but it limits direct comparison with
    the published implementation and prevents attributing local results to that
    implementation unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Level weights.} Whenever possible, an intervention and its
    baseline use the same objective weights. H-CAST and HT-CapsNet expose
    unweighted level losses to lexicographic mode, whereas the native HT-CapsNet
    baseline uses dynamic weighting; comparisons that cannot match this variable
    are labelled explicitly.
    \item \textbf{Training budget.} The native HT-CapsNet baseline uses 200
    epochs. Its explicit lexicographic configuration is limited to 100 because it
    evaluates and combines three gradients per batch. Such a pair is neither
    epoch- nor compute-matched and cannot isolate the gradient intervention.
    \item \textbf{Backbone and pretraining.} CIFAR experiments use a
    from-scratch wide residual network where supported, whereas fine-grained
    experiments commonly use ImageNet-pretrained backbones. Effects that change
    between these settings are reported as backbone-dependent.
    \item \textbf{Dataset coverage.} The three taxonomies differ substantially,
    and the unsupported family--dataset pairs listed in
    \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_classes} are not used to make claims about the published
    methods.
    \item \textbf{Cross-family comparison.} Model families also differ in
    backbone, optimiser, augmentation, input resolution and training budget.
    Cross-family tables compare complete implementations; claims about an
    intervention rely instead on matched comparisons within a family.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter search.} One family--dataset setting received an
    automated search over learning rate, weighting and transform choices; the
    others use published or preset values. This asymmetry affects cross-family
    rankings, although no intervention was tuned separately from its baseline.
    \item \textbf{Resource accounting.} The operation and storage costs of
    explicit lexicographic mode are derived in \cref{ssec:lex_cost}. The matched
    empirical comparison of the baseline, explicit lexicographic mode and the
    LH-projection is structured in \cref{sec:results_computational_cost}; its
    timing protocol and measurements remain to be completed. Until then, the
    chapter makes no empirical efficiency ranking between the mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Statistical scope.} Results are descriptive. Means include the
    sample standard deviation and seed count when multiple seeds exist; paired
    differences are computed per seed where possible. No significance test is
    applied, and the non-native cells of the inference grid are treated as
    diagnostics rather than candidates for post-hoc selection.
    \item \textbf{Incomplete coverage.} Not every supported configuration was
    run. Missing cells remain marked \tbd in their corresponding tables and are
    treated as unanswered questions rather than negative results.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reporting protocol}
\label{ssec:design_protocol}

The following rules are applied throughout the chapter.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{One mechanism at a time.} The main experiments vary a single
    intervention against a matched baseline.
    \item \textbf{Preconditions before mechanisms.} Required changes to
    Hier-COS are evaluated separately before the LH-projection is enabled. Their
    effect is never included in the delta attributed to the projection.
    \item \textbf{Native inference is the headline result.} Each family is
    reported under its native readout. Other cells of the inference grid are
    labelled as diagnostics and are not selected post hoc.
    \item \textbf{Both decoders are reported.} Independent and top-down results
    use checkpoints selected for their respective decoding modes
    (\S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}); a decoder is not treated as an intrinsic
    property of a model family.
    \item \textbf{Activation is checked from logs.}\par HCC is verified through
    \texttt{proj\_constraint\_alpha}. Gradient ordering is verified through the
    logged cosine similarities, removed norms and degeneracy-guard rate. A
    configuration name alone is not evidence that an intervention was active.
    \item \textbf{Metric direction is fixed.} FPA, the weighted accuracy summary wAP and
    per-level accuracy are higher-is-better; AHD and TICE are lower-is-better.
    Percentage differences are expressed in percentage points.
    \item \textbf{Comparisons are descriptive.} Every aggregate reports its seed
    count and, for multiple seeds, the sample standard deviation; paired
    differences are computed seed by seed where possible. No significance test
    is applied. A single-seed result, or a difference smaller than the observed
    seed-to-seed spread, is not used to claim stable superiority. Truncated epoch
    logs are excluded before curves are averaged.
    \item \textbf{A common figure set accompanies every trained mechanism.}
    Each mechanism is accompanied by the independent-decoding validation
    trajectories of FPA, TICE, wAP and AHD, with the mean and one sample
    standard deviation over seeds and the independently selected checkpoint
    marked on every curve; the per-run, per-level training-objective curves
    produced by \texttt{plot\_per\_run\_per\_level\_training\_losses()}; and
    test accuracy by hierarchy level at the independently selected checkpoint,
    produced by \texttt{plot\_final\_level\_test\_accuracy()}. The plotted
    \texttt{loss\_level\_*} values are the configured contributions to that
    run's objective. They support comparisons across depth within one arm, but
    not numerical comparisons between arms whose objectives differ. The main
    section retains the views needed for its argument; redundant endpoint and
    checkpoint-selection views are placed in the supplementary-figures
    appendix.
    \item \textbf{Constraint mechanisms receive a tailored diagnostic.} HCC is
    diagnosed by constraint activation, score displacement, argmax flips and
    ground-truth parent mass; explicit lexicographic training by pre- and
    post-projection gradient cosines, removed norm and guard skips; and the
    LH-projection by its singular spectrum, effective rank and remaining
    representation headroom. Direct subspace supervision changes only the
    objective and therefore uses the common metric, loss and level-accuracy
    figures without an additional mechanism-specific diagnostic. FPA--TICE
    trade-off figures are reserved for the later synthesis and are not part of
    this common per-mechanism set.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experiment overview}
\label{sec:results_campaign}

The results are presented in the following order:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \Cref{sec:results_baselines} establishes the unmodified reference for
    each family.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_inference} applies alternative readouts, HCC and both
    decoders to frozen baseline checkpoints only. This establishes the
    inference-time effects before trained interventions are considered.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_hcc,sec:results_subspace,sec:results_lex} compare
    three directly trained interventions with their matched baselines, ordered by
    where each acts: on the outputs, on the objective, then on the gradient.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_substrate} changes the Hier-COS frame and normaliser
    once, establishing the LH-compatible structural configuration and measuring what
    the change costs. Everything before it is read at the published dense,
    globally normalised configuration.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_lhprojection} reports the LH-projection on that
    configuration: first against explicit lexicographic mode, then crossed with the
    level weighting, whose interaction is measured on each arm rather than
    transferred between them, and finally under the CIFAR-100 backbone-capacity
    ladder.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_mechanisms} places the four mutually exclusive
    interventions in one comparison.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_inference_priority} returns to the inference grid on
    the explicit lexicographic and LH-projection checkpoints, after both
    mechanisms and their matched comparison have been established.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_computational_cost} compares model size and training
    cost for the matched baseline, explicit lexicographic and LH-projection arms.
    \item \Cref{sec:results_summary} summarises the supported conclusions and
    names any remaining limitations locally.
\end{enumerate}

The target protocol of \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_protocol} uses three seeds per
thesis-table row. Each table reports its realised seed count, in the $n$ column
where the count varies by row and in the caption otherwise; incomplete cells
remain explicitly marked until the campaign is closed. Which configurations a
family does not admit at all is fixed by Table~\ref{tab:framework_support};
supported configurations that were not run are treated in
the corresponding result section rather than interpreted as negative evidence.

\section{Unified baselines}
\label{sec:results_baselines}

This section gives the no-mechanism reference for H-CAST, LH-DNN, HRN and
Hier-COS on the common splits and taxonomies of \S\ref{sec:datasets}. Here
\emph{baseline} means that neither HCC nor explicit lexicographic gradient
projection is active; everything else --- architecture, objective, optimiser,
augmentation and preprocessing --- remains as the family defines it. For
Hier-COS the baseline is the matched reference used throughout the chapter,
global-softmax cross-entropy with regularisation and \texttt{kl\_leaf} weights
rather than the paper's node-wide KL objective; the cost of the further
adaptations that the LH-projection requires is measured separately
(\S\ref{ssec:design_substrate}). Every family--dataset cell aggregates the three
seeds of \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_protocol}. HT-CapsNet is omitted from the section:
only one seed of its CIFAR-100 baseline completed
(\S\ref{sec:results_campaign}), which is below the reporting standard adopted
here, and its two remaining dataset cells are unsupported extrapolations
(\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_classes}).

The tables serve two purposes. Within a family, the rows
are controls for the mechanism experiments that follow: the arms of
\cref{sec:results_hcc,sec:results_lex,sec:results_subspace} are compared against
them seed by seed, and those paired differences are what the claims of this
chapter rest on. Across families the rows compare complete implementations
rather than isolated hierarchy mechanisms, since backbone, optimiser,
augmentation, input resolution and training budget all remain family-specific
(\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_families}). The two most consequential differences are
HRN's $448\times448$ fine-grained input, against $224\times224$ for the other
pretrained families, and LH-DNN's fifteen-epoch from-scratch schedule. A
cross-family ranking is therefore descriptive, and it is not a reproduction of
the published numbers: where each local implementation departs from its source
is recorded in \S\ref{ssec:fidelity_classes}, and no claim below depends on
agreement with a published score.

\subsection{Reading the results}
\label{ssec:results_baseline_reading}

The conventions stated here hold for every table and figure of this section and
are not repeated in the captions.

\paragraph{What a row contains.} Each row reports one family on one dataset
under its native readout --- node scores for H-CAST, HRN and LH-DNN, subspace
norm for Hier-COS (\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}) --- and under independent
decoding, in which every level is decoded from its own scores without reference
to the others. Top-down decoding is a separate quantity and is taken up in
\S\ref{ssec:results_decoder}. Entries are the mean over the three seeds $0$, $1$
and $2$, followed by the sample standard deviation over the same three runs.
Accuracies, wAP and TICE are percentages; AHD is in levels --- equivalently in
tree edges, as the corresponding figure panels are labelled --- and runs from
$0$ on a fully correct path to $3$ when even the coarsest level is wrong. The
arrow in each column header gives the direction of improvement. Within a column,
the best mean is bold and the second best is underlined, ties at the displayed
precision sharing the emphasis; the standard deviation is never emphasised.
Three repetitions support a dispersion estimate but no significance test, so the
emphasis is descriptive and does not assert a reliable difference
(\S\ref{ssec:design_threats}).

\paragraph{Which epoch a row comes from.} A row is neither the final epoch of
training nor the best epoch on test. It is the test evaluation of the single
checkpoint chosen on the validation split by the lexicographic criterion of
\S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}: candidate epochs are ordered by validation
FPA first, ties resolved by lower TICE and then by higher wAP, all three
computed in the same decoding mode as the row itself. Test data are never
consulted in that choice (\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_protocol}). TICE and wAP enter
only as tie-breaks, so unless two epochs are exactly equal in validation FPA the
selected epoch is the validation-FPA argmax and nothing else; the TICE and AHD
columns are consequently not quantities the rule optimises, and they can be read
as properties of the selected model rather than as artefacts of the selection.

\paragraph{Validation trajectories.}
\Cref{fig:baseline_validation_cifar,fig:baseline_validation_cub,fig:baseline_validation_aircraft}
show one dataset each in four panels: validation FPA and wAP above, TICE and AHD
below, against training epoch. A line is the mean over the three seeds at each
epoch, and the band around it is one sample standard deviation across those
seeds at that epoch --- a dispersion, not a confidence interval. The filled
circle on each line marks the epoch selected by the criterion above, that is,
the checkpoint whose test metrics form the corresponding table row; its height
is the validation value at that epoch, whereas the table reports the test value.
The marked epoch is not in general the last one and differs across families on
the same dataset, which is why the tables cannot be read as an end-of-schedule
comparison. Three of the four panels are exactly the quantities entering the
selection criterion, so the choice is auditable from the figure: it is visible
whether a selected point sits on a plateau or on an isolated spike. LH-DNN
curves stop at epoch $15$ because that is its configured schedule
(\S\ref{ssec:fidelity_families}), not because a run was truncated.

\paragraph{Level-resolved test accuracy.}
\Cref{fig:baseline_final_level_accuracy} reports test accuracy at those same
selected checkpoints, resolved by hierarchy level: one panel per dataset, one
line per family, points the mean over three seeds and error bars one sample
standard deviation. The horizontal axis is ordinal --- the three levels of that
dataset's taxonomy, coarse to fine --- so a segment joins the levels of a single
family and interpolates nothing; the shared vertical scale is what makes the
coarse-to-fine degradation comparable across the three panels.

\paragraph{Cross-dataset ranking.} Hier-COS leads CIFAR-100 with
$77.52\%$ FPA, whereas HRN leads CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft with $83.10\%$
and $83.25\%$; H-CAST is second on the first two datasets and third on Aircraft.
The ordering has to be read against the recipe caveat above: HRN's advantage on
the two fine-grained datasets is confounded with its $448$-pixel input, which
retains four times the pixel area available to the other pretrained families
(\S\ref{sec:dataset_analysis}). No attempt is made here to separate the two, and
the sections that follow avoid the question entirely by comparing each family
only with itself.

\paragraph{Differences across hierarchy depth.} Among the three
competitive families, the spread between the highest and the lowest mean
accuracy widens from $1.64$ to $8.68$~pp between the coarsest and the finest
level on CUB, and from $3.38$ to $12.25$~pp on Aircraft, whereas on CIFAR-100 it
does not widen at all, moving from $3.32$ to $2.67$~pp;
\cref{fig:baseline_final_level_accuracy} shows the same pattern as three lines
that stay together on the left and separate towards the right in the two
fine-grained panels only. On those two datasets the coarse level is close to
saturation for all three families ($96.85$--$98.49\%$ on CUB, $92.22$--$95.60\%$
on Aircraft), so nearly all of the difference between them is made at the fine
level; on CIFAR-100, where the coarse level is not saturated, the differences
are distributed across the levels instead. The per-level validation trajectories
behind these endpoints are reported in
\cref{fig:app_baseline_levels_cifar,fig:app_baseline_levels_cub,fig:app_baseline_levels_aircraft}.

\paragraph{LH-DNN on the fine-grained datasets.} LH-DNN performs substantially
better on CIFAR-100 than on the two fine-grained datasets, although its
$51.40\%$ fine accuracy and $45.57\%$ FPA remain below the other CIFAR-100
baselines. On CUB and Aircraft its fine accuracy falls to $5.41\%$ and
$7.13\%$ and its FPA to $2.33\%$ and $1.96\%$. What this supports is narrow: the
paper-aligned fifteen-epoch, from-scratch recipe does not transfer to the two
fine-grained datasets. Its curves in
\cref{fig:baseline_validation_cub,fig:baseline_validation_aircraft} are still
moving when that schedule ends, and a longer budget was not tested, so the
finding concerns the recipe as published rather than the architecture. The rows
are kept as within-family controls for the mechanism experiments and are
excluded from comparisons among the three leading baselines.

\paragraph{Accuracy and consistency.} Independent TICE
measures how often a family's own scores describe an admissible path, before any
decoder or constraint is asked to enforce one. Hier-COS is lowest on all three
datasets ($0.60$--$1.04\%$), followed by H-CAST ($5.24$--$9.09\%$) and HRN
($4.72$--$15.71\%$), while LH-DNN reaches $63.05\%$ on CUB and $80.71\%$ on
Aircraft. The axis is not a restatement of accuracy in either direction. On
Aircraft, HRN is $3.60$~pp above Hier-COS in fine accuracy ($85.15$ against
$81.55$) and about eight times higher in TICE ($6.59$ against $0.78$).
Conversely, a low TICE does not make the remaining errors mild: on CUB, Hier-COS
has simultaneously the lowest TICE ($0.60\%$) and the highest AHD ($0.366$
levels) of the three competitive families. This is the empirical counterpart of
the coverage argument of \S\ref{ssec:inference_metrics}: accuracy, consistency
and severity are reported separately because none of them determines the others.

\paragraph{Fine accuracy and full-path accuracy.} The distance between
$\mathrm{Acc}_3$ and FPA counts the samples that are correct at the finest level
and wrong somewhere above it, and it is bounded by the consistency column. If a
decoded path is consistent and its fine label is correct, then each coarser
label is the image of that fine label under the parent maps and therefore equals
the corresponding ancestor of the ground-truth path, which is itself consistent
by construction (\S\ref{sec:hierarchy_construction}); the whole path is then
correct. Every sample counted by $\mathrm{Acc}_3$ but not by FPA consequently
has an inconsistent path, so $\mathrm{Acc}_3-\mathrm{FPA}\le\mathrm{TICE}$ in
every row. The three tables satisfy the bound, and how much slack they leave is
itself informative: the two columns differ by at most $0.28$~pp for Hier-COS, by
$1.82$ to $2.46$~pp for H-CAST and by $1.90$ to $6.68$~pp for HRN, the widest
case being HRN on CIFAR-100, where $75.13\%$ fine accuracy yields $68.45\%$ FPA.

\paragraph{Training trajectories.} The per-level training curves in
\cref{fig:baseline_losses_cifar,fig:baseline_losses_cub,fig:baseline_losses_aircraft}
complete the common figure set for the baselines. They document that each
configured level objective was optimised and expose the relative difficulty of
the three levels inside a run. They are not a loss ranking between families:
each family defines, regularises and scales its level terms differently. The
effect of the decoder, of the readout and of each trained mechanism on these
same checkpoints is deferred to the sections that introduce them.

\subsection{Constraint diagnostics}
\label{ssec:results_hcc_diag}

The metrics above record the outcome; the diagnostics of
\cref{ssec:inference_metrics} record the mechanism. Four quantities are read
here: the logged \texttt{proj\_constraint\_alpha}, which establishes that the
constraint was in force for the reported run; the projection deltas, which
measure how far the
correction displaces the fine-level scores and how often it flips the fine
argmax; the ground-truth parent mass, which \cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}
identifies as the quantity the correction can move; and the rank of the
ground-truth class among its siblings, which the same corollary shows to be
invariant and which therefore serves as a control --- a change in it cannot be
attributed to the projection.

unconstrained baseline checkpoints of the three datasets, projecting the fine

$1.0$. At the coarse-to-middle transition, HRN's displacement is about $1.0$ to
$1.5$ times the middle head's standard deviation, compared with $0.3$ to $0.7$
for H-CAST. Among the evaluated families, HRN's emitted scores therefore lie
furthest from the cascade constraint. These measurements support the
attachment-point interpretation in \S\ref{ssec:hcc_family_adaptation}; they are

\section{Supervision of taxonomy-subspace scores}
\label{sec:results_subspace}

Direct subspace supervision moves the loss from the taxonomy-node coordinates
onto the subspace norms the decoder ranks (\S\ref{sec:methodology_subspace}). The
mechanism is inactive at inference, so the readout and both decoders are those of
the baseline row and any difference measured below is a difference in what was
learned.

The mechanism is applicable to every family compared here. The subspace norm is
definable on all of them (\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}), and so therefore is
supervision of it (\S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting}); nothing in the objective is
specific to the fixed-frame family. It is nevertheless run on Hier-COS alone,
and the restriction is a decision about the campaign rather than a limit of the
mechanism. Hier-COS is the most favourable host available: its readout is native
and its coordinate magnitudes are calibrated by the objective itself, whereas the
classifier-head families reach the same scores only through their own output
activations, whose magnitudes nothing in their training calibrates
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}). The arm is negative on that most favourable
host, at a substantial accuracy cost and with no compensating gain
(\S\ref{ssec:results_subspace_summary}), so it was not extended to hosts where
the objective would start from a weaker footing. This is an unrun arm, not an
inapplicable one.

The arm and its baseline share the disabled label smoothing, mixup and cutmix that
\S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting} requires, or the contrast is confounded with the
augmentation; they share the level weighting by construction, since the
mechanism reuses the one the native objective applies. All nine completed
direct-subspace runs use $\tau=0.1$ and the dense random orthonormal frame, with
three matched seeds per dataset. The direct objective replaces the native loss
in full, including its coordinate regulariser, so the comparison estimates the
complete intervention and does not separate the induced target profile from the
removal of that regulariser.

\subsection{Sources of accuracy loss}
\label{ssec:results_subspace_depth}

The target margin decreases from $1.000$ at the coarsest level to $0.423$ and
$0.184$ at the middle and fine levels (\cref{prop:subspace_margin}).
The three accuracy columns of
Tables~\ref{tab:results_subspace_cifar}--\ref{tab:results_subspace_aircraft}
therefore resolve the effect by depth rather than reporting only the leaf
endpoint.

The cost is concentrated at depth. On CIFAR-100 the accuracy change becomes
progressively more negative from coarse to fine. On CUB, the objective improves
the first two levels by $0.52$ and $0.86$~pp but loses $1.70$~pp at the leaf;
on Aircraft it improves the coarse level by $0.40$~pp, leaves the middle level
effectively unchanged and loses $1.42$~pp at the leaf. This pattern is
consistent with the shrinking attainable margin making the fine decision the
binding one, but it does not establish that explanation: the three margins are
shared by all datasets, and the intervention also replaces the native
coordinate regulariser. Supplementary
Figure~\ref{fig:app_subspace_final_level_accuracy} shows the same concentration
at depth in absolute rather than delta form; it is kept outside the main section
because the same values are already reported in the tables.

\subsection{Temperature}
\label{ssec:results_subspace_tau}

The temperature rescales the depth-dependent margins of
\cref{prop:subspace_margin} inside the softmax, which is why
\S\ref{ssec:design_objective} treats it as an axis of the mechanism rather than
a constant to be selected from a single result. The only full runs available
use $\tau=0.1$; runs at other temperatures are smoke tests and do not satisfy
the three-seed reporting protocol.
Tables~\ref{tab:results_subspace_cifar}--\ref{tab:results_subspace_aircraft}
therefore identify a negative result for the specified $\tau=0.1$ condition,
not a temperature-robust negative result for direct subspace supervision as a
whole. At least two additional temperatures bracketing $0.1$, evaluated with
the same matched seeds, are required to identify the sensitivity and determine
whether the tested value is an interior setting or a boundary of a monotone
trend. Until that sweep exists, the temperature question remains unidentified.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{ssec:results_subspace_summary}

The arm is complete on its own terms and its result is negative. Supervising the
ranked quantity directly, at $\tau=0.1$ and on all three datasets, costs
independent FPA ($3.38\pm0.28$, $2.37\pm0.32$ and $1.77\pm0.23$~pp), raises AHD,
and raises TICE rather than trading one for the other. The cost is concentrated
at the leaf, where the attainable margin is smallest, and the coarse levels are
unharmed or slightly improved on CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft. Aligning the
trained and ranked functions is therefore not sufficient on its own to improve
the ranking, at least in the form tested here.

Three limits bound that statement, and none is a missing run that the campaign
intends to add. The objective replaces the native loss in full, including its
coordinate regulariser, so the estimate is of the complete intervention rather
than of the target profile alone. The temperature remains a single point
(\S\ref{ssec:results_subspace_tau}), so the negative result is identified for
$\tau=0.1$ and not for the mechanism at every scale. And the finding rests on one
family. The mechanism is applicable to all of them, but only Hier-COS was run, so
whether the same cost appears where the coordinates come from a classifier's
output activation rather than from a calibrated frame is untested --- untested by
choice, given the result here, rather than by obstacle.

\section{Gradient-space lexicographic optimisation}
\label{sec:results_lex}

Lexicographic mode imposes coarse-to-fine priority on the assembled gradient
update once per batch and has no direct effect at inference
(Table~\ref{tab:framework_mechanisms}). A change in TICE is therefore a change
in what the model learned. For Hier-COS the configured coefficients remain
inside the three exposed level losses and therefore scale the gradients supplied
to lexicographic composition. Every comparison below consequently uses a
baseline with the same numerical coefficients, so the coefficients are held
matched rather than varied: the weight cross of \cref{ssec:results_lex_scaling}
is run on the LH arm only, so the interaction between the coefficients and
\emph{this} locus is not identified by the campaign. Other families
follow the adaptations of \cref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}. HRN carries no
analogous level coefficients, and its three exposed terms sum to the objective
its baseline optimises, so its row isolates the projection alone.

Every Hier-COS row in this section is read at the dense random orthonormal frame
and the global normaliser, the configuration in which the family was published
and the one shared with \cref{sec:results_hcc,sec:results_subspace}. The
LH-projection cannot be trained there, so the frame and normaliser are changed
once, in \cref{sec:results_substrate}, and every result requiring the changed
configuration is reported in \cref{sec:results_lhprojection}. That includes the
level-weight cross, which is run on the LH arm alone
(\cref{ssec:results_lex_scaling}) and therefore says nothing about how the
lexicographic arm would behave at another rule. At the single leaf-heavy rule
these rows are read at, the two levels holding priority on CIFAR-100 end
substantially under-fitted and consistency degrades rather than improves; that
observation comes from the per-level training signal of
\cref{ssec:results_lex_diag}, not from a weight sweep, so it describes the
reported setting and is not evidence of a monotone trend in the weighting. The
rows below are therefore the published-configuration lexicographic result for
Hier-COS at one weighting, not the whole of it.

\section{Hier-COS ablations}
\label{sec:results_substrate}
\label{sec:results_ablations}

The Hier-COS rows of \cref{sec:results_hcc,sec:results_subspace,sec:results_lex}
are all read at the dense random orthonormal frame and the global normaliser, the
configuration in which the family was published. That is the correct setting
for a claim about the published model, it is the only one on which the ordering
control of \S\ref{ssec:results_lex_ordering} is available at matched weighting,
and holding it fixed is what makes the four mechanisms comparable in
\cref{sec:results_mechanisms}.

The LH-projection cannot be trained on it. It requires a frame that does not mix
levels and a per-level normaliser, so that each level loss depends on its own
branch alone (\S\ref{ssec:design_substrate}). This section changes those two
components once, and only once, and measures what the change costs before any LH
row is reported. The frame ladder first identifies the best frame on which
independent level heads remain admissible; the softmax ablation then measures
the cost of replacing the global node-wide normaliser by per-level normalisation
at that frame. Together they establish one structural configuration per dataset,
carried unchanged through \cref{sec:results_lhprojection}. The model's residual
transform is held fixed throughout. The two remaining Hier-COS ablations are
reported elsewhere for reasons of attribution: the level-weight cross, run on the
LH-projection arm alone (\cref{ssec:results_lex_scaling}), because the weighting
accumulates into the reserved subspace under that locus and not under the
explicit one, so a rule fixed on either arm cannot be carried to the other; and
the backbone-capacity ladder at the end of the LH-projection section
(\cref{ssec:results_backbone_capacity}), because it varies the feature extractor
only after the reference LH setting has been fixed.

Two consequences are recorded here rather than assumed. The selected
configuration differs across datasets, so it is carried as a column on every
table that follows. And because a change of frame or normaliser could move the
same metrics that \cref{sec:results_lex} attributes to gradient ordering, no
lexicographic result is transferred across this boundary: the
published-configuration rows and the structural ones are stated separately and
related only through the matched within-cell contrasts of
\cref{sec:results_lhprojection}.

\section{In-graph priority with LH-projection}
\label{sec:results_lhprojection}

The LH-projection imposes the same coarse-to-fine priority as
\cref{sec:results_lex}, but inside the computational graph at a branch point
rather than on the assembled gradient after backpropagation
(\S\ref{ssec:design_locus}). It is reported here because it requires the
structural configuration established in \cref{sec:results_substrate}; every row
below
therefore carries the adaptation cost measured in
\cref{ssec:results_frame,ssec:results_softmax}. No arm is compared with the
dense-frame, globally normalised baseline of \cref{sec:results_baselines}.

On both host families, enabling the LH-projection also enables the structural
changes the mechanism requires, so every row identifies the complete adaptation
package rather than the projection operator alone; that limitation is restated
wherever a row is interpreted.

The section is organised by host. It opens with the choice of the two hosts. The
Hier-COS arm follows: the direct comparison against explicit lexicographic mode
at matched frame, normaliser and weighting; then the level weights evaluated on
the LH arm alone, because the coefficients accumulate into the reserved subspace
under this locus and not under the explicit one; then why a weighting rule
established on the lexicographic arm cannot be carried to the LH arm. The HRN arm follows, where the package is not
decomposable and no ablation ladder exists. The section closes with the
numerical and dimensional conditions the projection requires on both hosts.

\subsection{Host model families}
\label{ssec:results_lhproj_hosts}

Of the five families, three cannot host the mechanism at all: H-CAST has no
single branch point, HT-CapsNet chains its level capsules and scores them by
capsule norm rather than a linear map, and LH-DNN already carries the projection
as part of its architecture (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting}). The mechanism is
therefore available on Hier-COS and HRN, and on no others.

That the two admissible families are also the strongest baselines under the
unified protocol is what makes the arm worth running rather than merely possible.
Across
\cref{tab:results_baseline_cifar,tab:results_baseline_cub,tab:results_baseline_aircraft},
Hier-COS and HRN hold the best row on every metric and dataset with a single
exception: H-CAST ties HRN on CUB-200-2011 coarse accuracy at $98.49$. Hier-COS
leads CIFAR-100 outright and holds the best TICE on all three datasets
($1.04$, $0.60$ and $0.78$), while HRN leads CUB-200-2011 and FGVC-Aircraft on
$\mathrm{Acc}_3$, FPA and AHD ($85.06$/$83.10$/$0.242$ and
$85.15$/$83.25$/$0.303$). H-CAST is second on CIFAR-100 and on CUB-200-2011, so
the admissible set is not simply the strong set; but H-CAST is excluded
structurally --- it has no single branch point --- rather than by selection, and
LH-DNN, the weakest baseline by a wide margin on all three datasets, is the one
family for which the mechanism is native.

This matters for what a deficit would mean. A mechanism that trades accuracy for
consistency is most informative where the accuracy it might spend has already
been established, and least informative on a family whose baseline is not
competitive, since a deficit there could not be separated from the baseline's own
weakness. Hier-COS additionally enters with the lowest TICE of the field, which
is the metric the mechanism is meant to improve, so the arm is tested against a
strong incumbent on its own target rather than against a permissive one. The two
families are also read under different native rules --- \texttt{subspace\_norm}
for Hier-COS and \texttt{node\_score} for HRN --- so the pairing varies the
readout as well as the architecture; \cref{ssec:results_readout} is where the
contribution of the readout rule itself is separated.

The two hosts are complementary in what they can identify, and this bounds what
the section concludes. The Hier-COS package substitutes components of a short
output path, and each substitution --- frame, normaliser, width --- is separately
measurable, which is why \cref{ssec:results_frame,ssec:results_softmax} exist and
why the LH rows can be read against an adapted rather than a published baseline.
The HRN package instead reshapes a branch structure through three structural
steps (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hrn}): relocating the existing pool and adding the
shared map exposes a tractable common vector, direct affine heads make the
stacked matrix explicit without redundant linear factors, and the detached
score-space advantage retains coarse-to-fine conditioning while keeping each
level gradient branch-local. The package is applied as one arm, so the
HRN arm carries no ablation ladder and its rows identify the package as a whole.
A difference on HRN therefore locates an effect in the package; only the Hier-COS
arm can begin to locate it within one.

\subsection{Level weights and gradient scale}
\label{ssec:results_lex_scaling}

The frame and normaliser are fixed at the far rung established above: block and
per-level softmax on CIFAR-100, identity and per-level softmax on CUB-200 and
Aircraft. The cross is run on the LH arm alone: the weight rules are evaluated
under the LH-projection, and neither the unmodified nor the
\texttt{coarse\_first} arm is re-run at each rule.

The restriction follows the argument of
\S\ref{ssec:results_lhproj_weight_transfer}. Explicit lexicographic mode rebuilds
its reference from the current gradients at every step and normalises by that
reference's own inner product, so a weight changes the magnitude a level
contributes but not the subspace it is projected against. The LH-projection
instead projects against the previous levels' head weights, which are an
accumulated object: the coefficients change how the coarser heads train and
therefore change the reserved subspace at every subsequent step. The weighting
can consequently compound with the mechanism in the in-graph arm and cannot in
the explicit one, which is where the ablation is spent. In the decomposed
Hier-COS objective the coefficients multiply the classification terms while the
per-level regularisation coefficient remains fixed; unweighted classification
losses are therefore retained in the analysis rather than reconstructed from the
scalar total alone.

The cost of the restriction is stated rather than worked around. With no
baseline or lexicographic row at each rule, a difference \emph{across} rules
within the LH arm cannot be separated into a main effect of the weighting, which
any arm would show, and an interaction between the weighting and the projection.
The cross therefore identifies whether the LH arm is sensitive to the weighting
and at which rule it performs best; it does not identify that sensitivity as
belonging to the mechanism. Attribution to the mechanism would require the
matched rows this design omits, and no such attribution is made below.

\subsection{Weighting across optimisation loci}
\label{ssec:results_lhproj_weight_transfer}

The two mechanisms project against different references, and the difference is
structural rather than numerical. Explicit lexicographic mode projects each level
gradient off the summed higher-priority gradients, normalising the coefficient by
the reference's own inner product; the projection is therefore scale-invariant in
its reference and is rebuilt from the current gradients at every step. Rescaling
a level cannot change the projection at that step, so under explicit ordering the
level weights have no memory. The LH-projection instead removes, from each
level's input feature, the component lying in the span of the previous levels'
head weights. That reference is an accumulated object: the level weights change
how the earlier heads train, hence the protected subspace at every subsequent
step, so under the in-graph formulation the weights have full memory.

The consequence for the campaign is twofold. A weighting rule selected on the
lexicographic arm is not evidence about the LH arm, so the LH rows elsewhere in
this section state the weighting they were trained under rather than importing
one. And, in the other direction, the accumulation is what makes the ablation
worth running on this arm specifically, which is why
\cref{ssec:results_lex_scaling} crosses the weight rules with the LH-projection
and with no other arm.

\subsection{LH-projection on HRN}
\label{ssec:results_lhproj_hrn}

The second host tests whether the ordering established on Hier-COS survives a
change of architecture, readout and objective. HRN differs from Hier-COS on all
three: it scores by node score rather than subspace norm, trains a hierarchical
tree term with a separate leaf cross-entropy rather than a level softmax with a
coordinate regulariser, and its unmodified baseline carries substantially
larger nonlinear branches. The projected arm replaces those branches with direct
heads, so branch capacity is not held fixed. HRN also runs at batch size $8$
against Hier-COS's $64$, which
matters for the mechanism specifically: the projector is per-sample and its
guarantee degrades as the batch grows (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_limits}), so HRN is the
arm in which the projection is closest to the regime in which it is exact.

\subsubsection{Model changes}

The adaptation package is described in \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hrn}. It relocates
HRN's existing average pool before the split, inserts a shared linear map and
rectifier, replaces each complete native branch by one direct affine head, and
re-expresses the coarse-to-fine residual as a detached score-space advantage.
These substitutions are applied together. The comparison is
against the unmodified HRN baseline of \cref{sec:results_baselines} at matched
backbone, dataset, schedule and seeds.

Two consequences of the package should be carried into the reading of every row
below, because both are plausible sources of a deficit that has nothing to do
with the projection. First, replacing each RFM--FC path by one direct head
removes all per-level spatial processing and nonlinearity above the trunk and
reduces the branch parameter count. Second, the same replacement removes the
branch normalisation layers on which published HRN relies
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hrn}(ii)). Neither can be isolated within this arm. A row below the
baseline is therefore attributable to the package and not to the projection
operator, and the section does not claim otherwise.

\subsubsection{Ablation scope}

The Hier-COS arm is preceded by two structural ablations
(\cref{ssec:results_frame,ssec:results_softmax}) because its package decomposes:
the frame, the normaliser scope and the representation width are independent
substitutions, each measurable with the projection switched off. The HRN package
is evaluated only as a complete adaptation. Relocating the pool makes the
projected space tractable, the shared affine--rectifier pair supplies the
branch-point parameterisation, the direct heads supply the explicit matrices
$W_l$, and the score-space advantage retains the hierarchical forward
conditioning without coupling the level gradients. No matched intermediate arm
was run, so these effects cannot be separated empirically. The width axis of
\cref{ssec:results_lhproj_variants} has no analogue here because condition~4 is
met by the trunk width without a free parameter.

The consequence for identification is stated rather than worked around: within
the HRN arm, the projection operator and its adaptation package are
confounded. What the arm does provide is a second, architecturally independent
test of the direction of the effect, on a family whose baseline is competitive
under the unified protocol (\S\ref{ssec:results_lhproj_hosts}) and at a batch
size closer to the regime in which the projector's guarantee holds.

\subsection{Numerical and dimensional stability}
\label{ssec:results_lhproj_variants}

Two of the three qualifications of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_limits} can be probed
directly. The reserved subspace is built from
$A^{(l)}[b]=W_{<l}\operatorname{diag}(\rho'(k_b))$, so it is rebuilt per sample
from the PReLU derivative at that sample's own pre-activation
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(iii)); how much is removed along a reserved
direction is $1-\varepsilon/(\sigma_i^2+\varepsilon)$ and degrades as the
singular values of $A^{(l)}[b]$ approach zero, so the logged singular spectrum
is reported beside the metrics rather than assumed benign. The representation
width sets how much room exists above the reserved subspace: condition~4 of
\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting} requires $D>\sum_{l<L}C_l$, and at $D$ close to that
bound the complement the finest level is left to act in is nearly empty. The
advantage parameterisation of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(iii), in which each
head predicts a correction to its detached parent logit rather than an absolute
score, is carried as the third row because it changes what the reserved subspace
is reserved \emph{against}.

\subsection{Training time and peak memory}
\label{ssec:results_training_time}

Training time will be measured under a common hardware and software environment,
with matched batch size, numerical precision, data-loading configuration,
and software configuration. A dedicated ten-epoch run is used as a computational
benchmark rather than as a replacement training run. The first epoch warms up
the accelerator, data pipeline and memory allocator; mean epoch time is computed
over epochs 2--10. The timed region contains the training batches only, with
validation, logging and checkpoint I/O excluded, and the accelerator is
synchronised at its boundaries. This estimates mechanism overhead without
requiring the complete experiments to be repeated or conflating runtime with
the epoch at which a checkpoint is selected.

Peak accelerator memory is recorded during the same ten-epoch run after model,

The expected sources of overhead differ. The no-lex baseline obtains one
backward pass from the scalarised loss. Explicit lexicographic mode obtains three
level gradients from the retained graph and then projects and stores them as
described in \cref{ssec:lex_cost}. The LH-projection retains the ordinary
end-to-end optimisation schedule but adds the per-sample projection operations
inside the computational graph. The measured ratios, rather than the number of
backward calls alone, will determine the practical ranking.

Peak memory is especially informative for explicit lexicographic mode because
the three level-gradient tuples coexist before the optimiser update
(\cref{ssec:lex_cost}). It complements epoch time: the former measures whether
the mechanism reduces the feasible batch size, while the latter measures its
steady-state time overhead.
