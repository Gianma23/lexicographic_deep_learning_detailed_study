\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methods}

This chapter defines the four mechanisms compared in the experiments.
Section~\ref{sec:methodology_problem} fixes the task and notation. Lexicographic
mode, the LH-projection and HCC all give precedence to coarse taxonomic levels,
but act at different points in the computation. Direct subspace supervision
imposes no ordering and instead changes the quantity supervised by the
objective:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lexicographic mode} constrains the \emph{update}. Gradients are
    computed one level at a time, and each lower-priority contribution is
    projected against the resultant assembled from the levels above it
    (\S\ref{sec:methodology_lex}).
    \item The \textbf{LH-projection} constrains \emph{what each level learns
    from}. At the point where a level head reads the shared representation, the
    learning signal that head sends back is filtered so that it carries no
    component along the directions in which the heads above it currently read
    (\S\ref{sec:methodology_lhproj}).
    \item \textbf{Direct subspace supervision} constrains the \emph{objective}.
    The loss is applied to the taxonomy-subspace scores that the decoder ranks,
    instead of to the internal coordinates from which those scores are computed
    (\S\ref{sec:methodology_subspace}).
    \item The \textbf{Hierarchical Constraint Cascade} (HCC) constrains the
    \emph{outputs}. The coarse scores are produced first, and the finer ones are
    obtained as the solution of a linear problem that forces them to agree with
    the coarse ones (\S\ref{sec:methodology_hcc}).
\end{itemize}

The first three interventions listed above affect training only. Direct subspace
supervision uses the same scores that the Hier-COS decoder ranks at inference
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout}); its intervention consists in moving the loss
onto those scores.

HCC transforms the outputs themselves and remains part of the model at
inference. It requires a complete taxonomy and a level-by-level affine correction,
and can also be applied to an already trained model.

Section~\ref{sec:methodology_inference} defines how a trained network produces a
path under several inference rules. Section~\ref{sec:methodology_axis} then
compares the four mechanisms.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:methodology_problem}

The setting is the one introduced in \cref{sec:hc_formulation}. In the Silla and
Freitas classification of hierarchical problems~\cite{silla2011survey}, it is
$\langle T, S, FD\rangle$, that is, a tree-shaped hierarchy in which every
instance belongs to exactly one root-to-leaf path and is labelled down to a leaf.
The following subsections fix the notation and the optimisation problem used in
the remainder of the chapter.

\subsection{Taxonomy}
\label{ssec:problem_taxonomy}

A \emph{taxonomy} is a rooted tree $\mathcal{T}$ whose nodes are partitioned into
$L$ levels, ordered from the most general to the most specific and indexed
$1,2,\dots,L$ in that order. Level $l$ contains $C_l$ classes, and
$N:=\sum_{l=1}^{L}C_l$ denotes the total number of nodes. Two structural
assumptions are made throughout:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unique parent.} Every class at level $l+1$ has exactly one
    parent at level $l$. The parent map is written
    $\pi_l:\{1,\dots,C_{l+1}\}\to\{1,\dots,C_l\}$, and its matrix form is the
    incidence matrix of the transition $l\to l+1$,
    $M_l\in\{0,1\}^{C_l\times C_{l+1}}$, with
    $M_l[i,j]=1$ if and only if $\pi_l(j)=i$. Uniqueness of the parent is
    exactly the statement that every column of $M_l$ contains a single
    non-zero entry.
    \item \textbf{No childless parent.} Every class at level $l$ has at least one
    child at level $l+1$, so that each row of $M_l$ is non-zero. As shown in
    \cref{lemma:diag}, this is precisely the condition under which the HCC
    projection is well defined.
\end{enumerate}

Under these assumptions each leaf determines a unique root-to-leaf path, so a
label may equivalently be given as a leaf identifier or as the path
\[
y=(y_1,\dots,y_L),\qquad y_l\in\{1,\dots,C_l\},\qquad y_l=\pi_l(y_{l+1}).
\]
A path satisfying $y_l=\pi_l(y_{l+1})$ for all $l$ is called \emph{consistent};
the datasets provide consistent ground-truth paths by construction, whereas a
model's prediction doesn't have to be consistent, which is what the metrics of
\S\ref{ssec:inference_metrics} quantify. A model can be consistent without being
correct.

All datasets used in this thesis are given a three-level hierarchy, $L=3$. Some
methods, such as Hier-COS, are defined for arbitrary $L$ and the restriction is a property of the
experimental setting, not of the formulation.

\subsection{Classifier output interface}
\label{ssec:problem_scores}

A hierarchical classifier is represented by a parameterised map
$f_\theta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{U}$, where the output space $\mathcal{U}$
depends on the model architecture. To formulate the task independently of that
architecture, let
\[
\rho_l:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}^{C_l},
\qquad
z_l(x;\theta):=\rho_l\!\big(f_\theta(x)\big)
\]
be the score vector used for level $l$. The maps $\rho_l$ constitute the
model's readout. The problem formulation requires only one
score for each class at each level, it does not prescribe how the network must
produce those scores. The explicit dependence on $x$ and $\theta$ is omitted
whenever it is not needed. Levels are always indexed coarse-to-fine, so that
$z_1$ refers to the coarsest level and $z_L$ to the finest.

The compared families realise this interface differently. Models with
level-specific classifier heads, including H-CAST, LH-DNN, HRN and HT-CapsNet,
make the vectors $z_l$ available directly. Hier-COS instead produces a vector
$u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ of taxonomy-node coordinates and, under its native
readout, assigns node $c$ the score
\[
z_l[c]=\Big(\sum_{j\in I_{l,c}}u_j^2\Big)^{1/2},
\]
where $I_{l,c}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ indexes the taxonomy coordinates assigned
to that node: its ancestors, itself and its descendants.

This architectural difference is relevant later only because a method may
modify either the network's internal output or the scores used to make a
prediction.

\subsection{Scalarised objectives and level weights}
\label{ssec:problem_objective}

The baseline training objectives can be written, when they expose one loss per
level, as a weighted sum
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
=
\sum_{l=1}^{L} w_l\,\ell_l\big(z_l,y_l\big)
\;+\;
\lambda\,\mathcal{R}\big(z_1,\dots,z_L\big),
\]
where $\ell_l$ is a level-wise loss (cross-entropy, or a family-specific
surrogate such as the capsule margin loss of HT-CapsNet), $w_l>0$ are
coefficients with $\sum_l w_l=1$, and $\mathcal{R}$ is an optional
family-specific regulariser with coefficient $\lambda$. The normalisation of
$w$ fixes a common scale and does not change its interpretation: $w_l$
determines how much level $l$ contributes to the scalar objective. A larger
value makes that level more influential, but does not give it lexicographic
priority, since an improvement at one level can still compensate for a
deterioration at another. This is the role of level weights in architectures
based on a weighted-sum scalarisation, such as B-CNN~\cite{zhu2017bcnn}.

The compared model families do not all define or use these coefficients in the
same way. LH-DNN and H-CAST use an unweighted sum, HRN has no analogous fixed
level coefficient, and HT-CapsNet updates its level weights during training.
Other families assemble their native loss terms according to their own
definitions.

\subsection{Gradient scaling in lexicographic mode}
\label{ssec:problem_lr}

Lexicographic mode (\S\ref{sec:methodology_lex}) computes one gradient per level
and projects the lower-priority gradients before assembling the update. The
projection controls the directions in which a lower-priority objective may act,
but it does not remove the effect of gradient magnitude. Indeed, the projected level
contributions must still be added, and a much larger contribution can dominate
that sum. Relative gradient scale is therefore an empirical degree of freedom of
the lexicographic approach even though it does not change the priority order.

A first candidate is to set $\kappa_l=w_l$, reusing the coefficients of the
scalarised baselines. This provides a natural controlled comparison between the
two optimisation approaches. However, there is no reason for coefficients
defined or tuned for the scalarised objective to be optimal for lexicographic
mode. In the baseline they act through the direct sum
\[
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_l\nabla_\theta\ell_l,
\]
whereas lexicographic mode first modifies each lower-priority gradient according
to the higher-priority directions and only then combines the surviving
components. The projection can change their relative magnitudes, and the
sequential composition also depends on the scale of the intermediate
resultants. A vector that balances the unprojected gradient sum may therefore
leave the projected contributions poorly balanced.

The per-level factors $\kappa_l$ are introduced to study this point: they test
whether changing the relative magnitudes supplied to the projection makes
lexicographic training more effective than reusing the baseline weights.

For the decomposed Hier-COS objective used in this ablation,
\[
g_l(\kappa_l)=\nabla_\theta\!\left[\kappa_l\,\ell_l^{\mathrm{CE}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{R}_l\right],
\qquad
\Delta\theta=-\,\mathcal{A}\!\left(g_1(\kappa_1),\dots,g_L(\kappa_L)\right),
\]
where $\mathcal{A}$ denotes the projection and composition of
\S\ref{ssec:lex_modes}. The regulariser coefficient $\lambda$ remains fixed, so
changing $\kappa_l$ changes the balance between classification and regularisation
within that level as well as the magnitude of the level contribution.

Two new assignments of $\kappa$ are introduced, both defined directly from the
structure of the taxonomy. Introduce a virtual root at level $0$, with $C_0=1$,
and define the average branching factor into level $l$ as
\[
b_l:=\frac{C_l}{C_{l-1}},\qquad l=1,\dots,L.
\]
The two rules use these factors differently.

\subsubsection{Cumulative branching}

This rule measures the total expansion from
the root to level $l$. The product of the branching factors telescopes:
\[
q_l:=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}b_j
=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{l}\frac{C_j}{C_{j-1}}
=C_l.
\]
The resulting scale is normalised and equipped with an exponent $\beta$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_l^{\mathrm{cum}}(\beta)
&:=\frac{q_l^\beta}{\sum_{k=1}^{L}q_k^\beta}
=\frac{C_l^\beta}{\sum_{k=1}^{L}C_k^\beta}\\[0.4ex]
&\;=\operatorname{softmax}_l\!\big(\beta\log C\big),
\end{aligned}
\]
so cumulative branching is equivalent to scaling a level according to its
number of classes. The exponent controls the concentration: $\beta=0$ recovers
uniform scaling, $\beta=1$ gives the untempered class-count rule,
$0<\beta<1$ softens it, and $\beta>1$ sharpens it. The proposal considers
$\beta\in\{0.5,1,1.5\}$.

\subsubsection{Marginal branching}

This rule uses only the expansion introduced at
the current transition. Directly using $b_1=C_1$ for the coarsest level would
make the root-to-coarse expansion dominate the later factors. The coarsest level
is therefore taken as the reference unit, while each subsequent level retains
its local average branching factor:
\[
\begin{aligned}
s_1 &:= 1, \qquad
s_l := b_l=\frac{C_l}{C_{l-1}}\ \ (l>1), \\[0.6ex]
\kappa_l^{\mathrm{marg}} &:=\frac{s_l}{\sum_{k=1}^{L}s_k} .
\end{aligned}
\]
Unlike cumulative branching, this rule need not increase with depth: it assigns
the largest scale to the level whose transition has the largest average fan-out.

\subsection{The lexicographic problem}
\label{ssec:problem_priority}

The objective of \S\ref{ssec:problem_objective} is the one the baseline families
minimise, and it treats the $L$ levels as commensurable: a coefficient $w_l$
prices one level against another, so a gain at one level can always be bought
with a loss at some other. The problem this thesis addresses is instead the
lexicographic one, in which the levels are ordered by priority and the ordering
is absolute:
\begin{equation}
\lexmin_{\theta}\ \ \ell_1(z_1,y_1),\ \ell_2(z_2,y_2),\ \dots,\ \ell_L(z_L,y_L),
\label{eq:hc_lmop}
\end{equation}
This notation denotes a sequence of constrained problems: $\ell_1$ is minimised
first, and each loss $\ell_{l+1}$ is then minimised only over the minimisers of
$\ell_1,\dots,\ell_l$~\cite{fiaschi2024informed}. Accordingly, $\ell_1$ has the
highest priority and $\ell_L$ the lowest: coarse levels are processed before fine
ones.

The defining feature of \eqref{eq:hc_lmop} is that
a lower-priority objective may act only \emph{within the level set already fixed
by the higher-priority ones}. The priority mechanisms in this chapter
approximate or encode that restriction at different points of the computation.
Their guarantees are stated separately; none should be read as solving the
nested optimisation problem in \eqref{eq:hc_lmop} exactly.

As established in \cref{sec:lexico-real}, no positive real weight vector encodes a lexicographic
order (\cref{prop:debreu}), and for any fixed $w$ there are level-wise changes
under which the scalarised objective improves while the lexicographic order
worsens (\cref{cor:compensation}). The coefficients of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_objective} therefore express a trade-off between levels, not a lexicographic priority.

\paragraph{Order of priority.} The ordering in \eqref{eq:hc_lmop} is an
assumption rather than a consequence of the taxonomy. Under a
flat objective, predicting a different breed of the correct bird and predicting a
vehicle are the same event; a hierarchy exists precisely because they are not.

An error that preserves the high-level ancestors is less severe than one that
does not, which motivates placing coarse levels first. This argument ranks error
severity; it does not establish that using the same order during training will
reduce severe errors. The priority order is therefore a hypothesis, tested by a
fine-to-coarse control (\S\ref{ssec:design_controls}). Any effect reproduced when
the order is reversed cannot be attributed to the taxonomy. Coarse-level
protection may also restrict fine-level learning, so the results are interpreted
as an accuracy--consistency trade-off rather than as a uniform improvement.

\section{Gradient-space lexicographic optimisation}
\label{sec:methodology_lex}

Lexicographic mode imposes priority on the \emph{update direction} in parameter
space. The gradients of the level objectives are computed separately,
and each lower-priority gradient is orthogonalised against the current resultant
of the levels processed before it. This thesis refers to the procedure as
\emph{lexicographic mode}. It reads the priority ordering of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_priority} as a rule for constructing the gradient supplied to
the optimiser; \S\ref{ssec:lex_guarantee} states precisely what that rule does
and does not protect.

\subsection{Setting and requirements}
\label{ssec:lex_setting}

Lexicographic mode assumes a classifier with exactly three differentiable scalar
level objectives
\[
\ell_1(\theta),\quad \ell_2(\theta),\quad \ell_3(\theta),
\]
associated with the coarse, middle and fine levels and ordered by decreasing
priority: $\ell_1$ has the highest priority, followed by $\ell_2$ and then
$\ell_3$. Their parameter gradients are
\[
g_l := \nabla_\theta \ell_l(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{P}
\]
with respect to the trainable parameter vector $\theta$.

\paragraph{Requirement for level-wise losses.} Lexicographic mode combines the three per-level
gradients directly. Any objective term that cannot be assigned to a level
therefore lies outside this construction. This requires several model-specific
adaptations. H-CAST excludes its global KL term; HRN splits its native tree
objective into three conditional level terms and assigns leaf cross-entropy to
the fine level;
Hier-COS replaces its coupled node-wide KL objective with separate differentiable
level objectives; and HT-CapsNet uses its three level-wise capsule margin losses.
LH-DNN is excluded because it does not expose three independent differentiable
level objectives in the form required here. Its built-in branch-point projection
is treated separately in \S\ref{sec:methodology_lhproj}.

The implementation constructs the optimiser update exclusively from these three
exposed tensors. A term included only in a family's conventionally reported
scalar total, but not in one of the tensors $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$, is therefore
absent from a lexicographic step. This determines which parts of a native
objective survive the adaptation and which level coefficients affect the update.

\label{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}

that play the role of $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$ are not. Table~\ref{tab:lex_family_adaptation}
summarises the interface used by each architecture; the paragraphs below state
the objective-level consequences of each choice.

\paragraph{H-CAST.} The three objectives are the coarse, middle and fine
cross-entropies, each evaluated with its own level-wise softmax. The optional
global KL term is excluded because it is a single coupled loss over the
concatenated hierarchy and is not one of those three objectives. Although that
KL can be regrouped algebraically into level contributions, the contributions
would share one global normaliser: every resulting gradient would reach every
head and the staged support structure of Table~\ref{tab:lex_gradient_blocks}
would collapse to $P_{123}$. Omitting the term therefore preserves the property
of H-CAST that the experiment is intended to study.

H-CAST applies its level weights only when it assembles the scalar total; the
three tensors exposed to lexicographic mode are unweighted. Static mode gives
unit weights, whereas dynamic mode can change the reported scalar without
changing the optimiser update in this arm. The matched lexicographic setting
uses static unit weighting, and any comparison against a dynamically weighted
baseline must be identified as changing both the update rule and the relative
level scale.

\paragraph{Hier-COS.} The published-style \texttt{kl\_reg} objective supplies one
node-wide KL term and cannot be used directly. The
\texttt{global\_softmax\_ce\_reg} adaptation regroups the classification part
into one differentiable target term per level while retaining the global
normaliser; its sum is algebraically the same classification objective up to a
parameter-independent constant. The alternative
\texttt{level\_softmax\_ce\_reg} also normalises within each level and therefore
changes the objective rather than merely decomposing it. The main
lexicographic comparison uses the former unless softmax scope is itself the
variable under study.

For each level, Hier-COS exposes its weighted classification term plus that
level's coordinate regulariser. Its level coefficient is thus inside the
objective differentiated by lexicographic mode and, unlike the H-CAST and
HT-CapsNet coefficients, survives in the update; the regulariser coefficient
remains common across levels. A clean comparison uses the same decomposed loss,
frame and coefficients in the non-lexicographic control. Hier-COS's optional
LH-style branch projection is disabled because combining the in-graph and
assembled-gradient projections would apply two gradient interventions and
prevent attribution (\S\ref{ssec:design_axis}).

\paragraph{HT-CapsNet.} The three objectives are the unweighted capsule margin
losses computed from the length score at each routing stage. As in H-CAST, the
native static or dynamic level coefficients are applied only when the scalar
total is formed, so they do not enter a lexicographic step. In particular, the
dynamic-weight callback can continue updating its stored coefficients without
changing the update constructed from the three exposed losses. The
lexicographic arm therefore disables this ambiguity by using unit weighting; a
run that retained the native dynamic setting would still be, operationally, a
unit-weight lexicographic run.

\paragraph{HRN.} The native HRN objective does not expose three level
objectives, but it can be split into three without being changed. Its tree
branch emits one score $f_v\in(0,1)$ per taxonomy node, the sigmoid of the
corresponding head logit, and the objective is a distribution over a set
$\mathcal{S}$ of \emph{states}: one state per node $v$, namely the set of nodes
on the root-to-$v$ path, together with the empty state. A state's probability is
\[
P(s)=\frac{1}{Z}\exp\Big(\sum_{v\in s} f_v\Big),\qquad
Z=\sum_{s'\in\mathcal{S}}\exp\Big(\sum_{v\in s'} f_{v}\Big).
\]
Writing $\mathcal{D}(v):=\{s\in\mathcal{S}:v\in s\}$ for the states whose path
passes through $v$, that is $v$ together with its descendants, the paper-aligned
objective observes the leaf only:
\[
\ell^{\mathrm{tree}}=-\log P\big(\mathcal{D}(y_3)\big),
\qquad
\ell^{\mathrm{native}}=\ell^{\mathrm{tree}}+\ell^{\mathrm{leaf\text{-}CE}},
\]
with $\ell^{\mathrm{leaf\text{-}CE}}$ the cross-entropy of the separate
auxiliary leaf classifier.

The three observed nodes of one sample are nested,
$\mathcal{D}(y_1)\supseteq\mathcal{D}(y_2)\supseteq\mathcal{D}(y_3)$, so the
chain rule splits $\ell^{\mathrm{tree}}$ into three conditional negative
log-likelihoods. The \texttt{level\_conditional} adaptation takes these as the
level objectives and adds the auxiliary cross-entropy to the finest one:
\[
\ell_1=-\log P\big(\mathcal{D}(y_1)\big),\qquad
\ell_2=-\log \frac{P(\mathcal{D}(y_2))}{P(\mathcal{D}(y_1))},\qquad
\ell_3=-\log \frac{P(\mathcal{D}(y_3))}{P(\mathcal{D}(y_2))}
+\ell^{\mathrm{leaf\text{-}CE}}.
\]
Each term is non-negative and expresses one level's decision given the level
above it: which coarse subtree, which of its middle children, which leaf. The
sum telescopes,
\[
\ell_1+\ell_2+\ell_3=\ell^{\mathrm{native}},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
g_1+g_2+g_3=\nabla_\theta\ell^{\mathrm{native}} .
\]

As written, each conditional term is not yet a statement about its own level.
Both $P(\mathcal{D}(y_l))$ and the normaliser $Z$ sum over states of every
depth, and the two do not cancel: the numerator sums over one subtree, the
denominator over the whole state space. Writing $f^{(l)}$ for the scores of the
nodes at level $l$, the coarse term $\ell_1$ is therefore a function of
$f^{(3)}$ as well as of $f^{(1)}$, and the dependence is not a small residual
--- measured on the implementation, $\lVert\partial\ell_1/\partial
f^{(3)}\rVert$ is of the same order as $\lVert\partial\ell_1/\partial
f^{(1)}\rVert$. The consequence for a gradient-space method is direct: $g_1$
then carries substantial mass on the fine branch, whose parameters cannot move
the coarse logits at all. Ordering $g_3$ against such a $g_1$ would constrain
the fine head in directions that protect nothing, while leaving the
highest-priority objective free to drive that same head.

The level objectives are therefore evaluated with the levels finer than their
own held fixed. With $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ denoting the stop-gradient
operator, the identity in value and zero in derivative, $\ell_l$ is computed
from
\[
\big(f^{(1)},\dots,f^{(l)},\;\operatorname{sg}(f^{(l+1)}),\dots,\operatorname{sg}(f^{(3)})\big),
\]
so each term reads the finer scores as context but does not differentiate
through them. This finer-level detach acts on the objective, and is distinct
from the previous-level detach introduced later for the HCC projection, which
acts on representations.

Because $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ is the identity in value, the three terms
keep the values they had, so the telescoping identity and the gradient equality
above hold exactly as stated. What the detach changes is the support of the
level gradients, which becomes triangular: $g_l$ is supported on the parameters
of levels $1$ to $l$ together with the shared trunk, and on nothing finer.
Verified on the implementation, $g_1$ reaches the coarse branch and the trunk
only, $g_2$ adds the middle branch, and $g_3$ reaches every branch.

This support partition is what the ordering acts on, and it is not a separation
of the branches. The coupling that remains is genuine: the fine objective
reaches the coarse branch both through the cumulative embedding, in which the
coarse projection is a summand, and through the shared trunk, and those
parameters do determine the fine prediction. The lexicographic step orders that
coupling rather than removing it; removing it would leave three independent
networks sharing an input.

One limitation follows from the same construction. The detach acts on the
gradient and not on the value: the normaliser still sums over states of every
depth, so a step taken on the fine objective alone still moves the value of
$\ell_1$. The property established is that $\ell_1$ is stationary along its own
coordinates, not that the coarse objective is left unchanged.

Two consequences follow. First, with the projection disabled the arm reproduces
a paper-aligned HRN run exactly, so its matched baseline is the native model
itself and the projection is the only difference between the two; among the
families studied here HRN therefore permits the cleanest attribution of a
lexicographic effect, since no second change travels with the mechanism.
Second, treating the conditional terms as the level objectives remains a
modelling decision: HRN supervises the leaf alone, and the split names the
intermediate decisions its objective leaves implicit. As in the other families,
the auxiliary classifier belongs to the lowest-priority objective, so its
gradient is projected wherever it shares parameters with the coarse and middle
objectives.

\paragraph{LH-DNN.} No lexicographic arm is defined. LH-DNN's constitutive
branch-point operation already removes a higher-level component from a
lower-level signal. Adding the global parameter-gradient projection would apply
the same principle at a second location, while its native objective does not
provide the three independent tensors required by the present operator.

\subsection{Parameter blocks and the projection operator}
\label{ssec:lex_blocks}
\label{ssec:lex_projection}

Projecting one gradient onto another is meaningful only on coordinates reached
by both objectives. A projection coefficient estimated over the complete
parameter vector would otherwise mix shared parameters, on which objectives can
conflict, with level-specific parameters, on which no conflict exists. The
operator is therefore defined on exact gradient-support blocks.

For parameter coordinate $i$, let
\[
S(i):=\{\,l\in\{1,2,3\}:(g_l)_i\not\equiv0\,\}
\]
be the set of level objectives whose computational graphs reach that coordinate.
For each non-empty $A\subseteq\{1,2,3\}$, define
\[
P_A:=\{\,i:S(i)=A\,\}.
\]
A block is named by listing the elements of $A$, so that $P_{123}$, $P_{12}$,
$P_{23}$ and $P_{1}$ abbreviate $P_{\{1,2,3\}}$, $P_{\{1,2\}}$, $P_{\{2,3\}}$
and $P_{\{1\}}$; the names coincide with the diagnostic keys of
Table~\ref{tab:lex_gradient_blocks}.
The non-empty $P_A$ form a disjoint partition of the trainable coordinates
reached by at least one level objective. A block with $|A|\geq2$ is a set of parameters
where two or more objectives may propose conflicting updates. A singleton block $P_{l}$ contains no inter-objective
conflict, so its gradient passes through unchanged; it may nevertheless be
logged to analyse shared representation learning in a level-specific branch.

The two staged architectures illustrate why the general notation is needed.
H-CAST runs fine-to-coarse: below the fine branch all three losses act, whereas
after that branch only the coarse and middle losses remain. Its interacting
blocks are therefore $P_{123}$ and $P_{12}$; the
coarse-only block $P_{1}$ records movement beyond the middle branch.
HT-CapsNet has the reverse dependency: the middle capsule stage feeds the fine
stage, so its parameters lie in $P_{23}$, while the final capsule stage is
fine-only, $P_{3}$. Thus the same operator applies to both architectures,
but not on the same blocks.

For a block $B$ define the restricted inner product
$\langle u,v\rangle_B := \sum_{i\in B} u_i v_i$ and the induced norm
$\lVert u\rVert_B$. All projection coefficients below are computed with respect to these
restricted inner products, one block at a time.

\subsubsection{The projection operator}

Given a target gradient $g$, a reference gradient $r$ and a block $B$,
the basic operation is the Euclidean projection of $g$ onto the orthogonal complement of $r$,
restricted to $B$:

\[
\Pi_B(g;r)_i =
\begin{cases}
g_i - \gamma\, r_i, & i \in B,\\[1ex]
g_i, & i \notin B,
\end{cases}
\qquad
\gamma \;=\; \frac{\langle g,r\rangle_B}{\langle r,r\rangle_B} .
\]
The coefficient $\gamma$ is applied unconditionally, irrespective of the sign of
$\langle g,r\rangle_B$, so that
\[
\big\langle \Pi_B(g;r),\, r\big\rangle_B = 0 .
\]
The lower-priority objective is confined to the orthogonal complement of the higher-priority
direction, which is the parameter-space form of the level-set restriction of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_priority}: the subordinate objective may act only where the dominant one is,
to first order, indifferent.

One guard completes the definition. If
$\lVert r\rVert_B^2 \le \varepsilon$ the coefficient is ill-conditioned and the
projection is skipped, $\Pi_B(g;r):=g$, with $\varepsilon=10^{-12}$ throughout.

\subsection{Priority order}
\label{ssec:lex_modes}

With three objectives the pairwise operator has to be composed, and the
composition fixes what each level is made subordinate to. Two schemes are
defined; in both, the final update direction is the sum
$d := \tilde g_1 + \tilde g_2 + \tilde g_3$ of the (possibly projected) level
gradients.

\subsubsection{Coarse-to-fine priority}

On every selected block, retain the
highest-priority gradient present, then process the remaining gradients in
coarse-to-fine order. Each is orthogonalised against the resultant of the
already processed higher-priority directions on that block. For H-CAST this
specialises to the blocks $P_{123}$ and $P_{12}$: the coarse gradient is left
unchanged; the middle gradient is orthogonalised against it separately on
$P_{12}$ and $P_{123}$; and the fine gradient is orthogonalised on
$P_{123}$ against the
\emph{resultant} of the two higher-priority directions:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\tilde g_1 &:= g_1, \\
\tilde g_2 &:= \Pi_{P_{12}}\!\big(g_2;g_1\big) &&\text{on } P_{12}, \\
m &:= \Pi_{P_{123}}\!\big(g_2;g_1\big) &&\text{on } P_{123}, \\
\tilde g_3 &:= \Pi_{P_{123}}\!\big(g_3;\,g_1+m\big) &&\text{on } P_{123}.
\end{aligned}
\]
The reference for the fine level is $g_1+m$ rather than $g_1$ and $m$ separately: what the fine level is forbidden to oppose is the update the two levels above
it have jointly agreed on, not each of their directions in isolation.
On HT-CapsNet's two-objective block $P_{23}$, the same rule reduces to
$\tilde g_3=\Pi_{P_{23}}(g_3;g_2)$: the fine objective is subordinate to
the middle objective there, while $P_{3}$ passes through unchanged.

\subsubsection{Fine-to-coarse control}

The priority order is inverted and the same
block-local composition is applied. In the H-CAST specialisation the roles of $g_1$ and
$g_3$ are exchanged: the fine gradient is left unchanged, the middle
gradient is orthogonalised against it on $P_{123}$ (and left unchanged on
$P_{12}$, where the fine level
does not reach), and the coarse gradient is orthogonalised against the raw middle gradient on
$P_{12}$ and against the resultant $g_3+\tilde g_2$ on $P_{123}$.
On $P_{23}$ the corresponding control projects $g_2$ against $g_3$.

The two modes apply the same composition in reversed priority order, block by
block, over whichever levels reach that block. A selected $P_A$ performs
$|A|-1$ projections: two on a three-gradient block, one on a two-gradient
block, and none on a singleton. Every statement proved below for coarse-to-fine
priority therefore holds for the reversed order after exchanging the dominant
and subordinate objectives. The support blocks themselves do not change when
the priority is reversed; they are fixed by the architecture.
Only coarse-to-fine priority is discussed explicitly from here on.

\subsection{Guarantee and scope}
\label{ssec:lex_guarantee}

The guarantee is local: it concerns the change predicted for one sufficiently
small update. Consider first the idealised gradient step
$\theta^+=\theta-sd$, where $s>0$ is the step size and $d$ is the assembled gradient. Differentiability
of $\ell_l$ gives
\[
\ell_l(\theta^+)
=\ell_l(\theta)-s\,\langle g_l,d\rangle+o(s).
\]
Thus $\langle g_l,d\rangle$ with a positive value predicts a decrease in $\ell_l$, zero predicts no first-order
change, and a negative value predicts an increase. The purpose of the projection
is to control this inner product for the higher-priority objective.

The result depends on how many objectives reach the parameter block. Suppose
first that only a higher-priority gradient $g_{\mathrm{H}}$ and a lower-priority
gradient $g_{\mathrm{L}}$ reach a block $B$. After projection,
\[
m:=\Pi_B(g_{\mathrm{L}};g_{\mathrm{H}}),
\qquad
d=g_{\mathrm{H}}+m,
\qquad
\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},m\rangle=0.
\]
Consequently, adding the lower-priority contribution $m$ does not change
$\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},d\rangle$: the predicted decrease of the
higher-priority objective is exactly the one produced by $g_{\mathrm{H}}$ alone.

When three objectives reach the block, the final projection has a different
reference. The middle gradient is first projected against $g_1$, giving $m$;
the fine gradient is then projected against the accumulated direction
$h=g_1+m$. It is therefore made orthogonal to $h$, but not necessarily to $g_1$
and $m$ separately. The proposition makes this distinction precise, and
\cref{fig:lex_projection_geometry} shows it geometrically.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:lex_firstorder}
Fix a selected block $B$ on which the degeneracy guard of
\S\ref{ssec:lex_projection} is inactive. In this proposition all gradients,
inner products and norms are restricted to $B$, and $d$ denotes the direction
assembled on that block under coarse-to-fine priority.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{(a) Two levels reach $B$.} Denote their gradients by
$g_{\mathrm{H}}$ and $g_{\mathrm{L}}$, for the higher- and lower-priority level,
respectively. If
$m:=\Pi_B(g_{\mathrm{L}};g_{\mathrm{H}})$ and
$d=g_{\mathrm{H}}+m$, then
\[
\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},d\rangle
  =\lVert g_{\mathrm{H}}\rVert^{2}.
\]

\medskip
\noindent\emph{(b) Three levels reach $B$.} Here $d=g_1+m+\tilde g_3$ with
\[
m:=\Pi_B(g_2;g_1),
\qquad
h:=g_1+m,
\qquad
\tilde g_3:=\Pi_B(g_3;h),
\]
and let $\rho:=\langle\tilde g_3,g_1\rangle$. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\langle h,d\rangle   &=\lVert h\rVert^2
                       =\lVert g_1\rVert^{2}+\lVert m\rVert^{2},\\[0.4ex]
\langle g_1,d\rangle &=\lVert g_1\rVert^{2}+\rho.
\end{aligned}
\]
The enforced orthogonalities do not constrain the sign of $\rho$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Both parts use only the orthogonalities the operator enforces by construction.

(a) Since $\langle m,g_{\mathrm{H}}\rangle=0$,
\[
\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},d\rangle
=\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},g_{\mathrm{H}}+m\rangle
=\lVert g_{\mathrm{H}}\rVert^2.
\]

(b) The construction gives $\langle m,g_1\rangle=0$ and
$\langle\tilde g_3,h\rangle=0$. Therefore
$\langle h,d\rangle=\langle h,h+\tilde g_3\rangle=\lVert h\rVert^2$;
orthogonality of $g_1$ and $m$ gives
$\lVert h\rVert^2=\lVert g_1\rVert^2+\lVert m\rVert^2$. Finally,
$\langle g_1,d\rangle=\lVert g_1\rVert^2+\rho$, again because
$\langle g_1,m\rangle=0$. Although
$\langle\tilde g_3,g_1+m\rangle=0$, this condition only requires
$\langle\tilde g_3,g_1\rangle=-\langle\tilde g_3,m\rangle$ and hence does not
fix the sign of either term.
\end{proof}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/methodology/lex_projection_geometry.pdf}
\caption{The block-restricted projection and its composition, drawn to scale in
the plane spanned by $g_1$ and $m$. \textbf{(a)} On a block reached by two
levels, $m=\Pi_B(g_{\mathrm{L}};g_{\mathrm{H}})$ is orthogonal to
$g_{\mathrm{H}}$, so adding it leaves $\langle g_{\mathrm{H}},d\rangle=\lVert
g_{\mathrm{H}}\rVert^{2}$: the decrease predicted for the higher-priority
objective is the one its own gradient produces. \textbf{(b)} On a block reached
by three levels, $\tilde g_3=\Pi_B(g_3;h)$ is orthogonalised against the

therefore keeps a component along $g_1$ equal and opposite to its component
along $m$, so $\langle g_1,d\rangle=\lVert g_1\rVert^{2}+\rho$ with
$\rho=\langle\tilde g_3,g_1\rangle=-\langle\tilde g_3,m\rangle$. The dotted accent arrow is the
opposite orientation on $h^{\perp}$, equally admissible and giving $\rho>0$:

exchanged.}
\label{fig:lex_projection_geometry}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Reading part (a).}
On a two-level block $B$, the lower-priority component leaves the contribution
of $B$ to the higher-priority directional derivative unchanged.
Under coarse-to-fine priority, $g_{\mathrm{H}}=g_1$ and
$g_{\mathrm{L}}=g_2$ on $P_{12}$, while $g_{\mathrm{H}}=g_2$ and
$g_{\mathrm{L}}=g_3$ on $P_{23}$. No inequality between abstract indices is
needed. There is no symmetric guarantee for the lower-priority objective,
because
\[
\langle g_{\mathrm{L}},d\rangle
=\langle g_{\mathrm{L}},g_{\mathrm{H}}\rangle+\lVert m\rVert^2
\]
has no fixed sign: the first term can be negative and larger in magnitude than
the second. Thus a higher-priority direction is allowed to increase a
lower-priority objective.

\paragraph{Reading part (b).}
On a three-level block, the guarantee is weaker. The final fine-level component
does not change the component of $d$ along the accumulated direction $h$:
since $d=h+\tilde g_3$ and $\tilde g_3\perp h$, the orthogonal projection of
$d$ onto the span of $h$ is $h$ itself. Equivalently,
$\langle h,d\rangle=\lVert h\rVert^2$.

$h$ is not in general the gradient of a level objective. The residual
$\rho=\langle\tilde g_3,g_1\rangle$ can have either sign, so the fine component
can reduce $\langle g_1,d\rangle$ below $\lVert g_1\rVert^2$ and can even make it
negative. To see that this can arise from the stated construction, take the raw
gradients
\[
g_1=(1,0),\qquad g_2=(0,1),\qquad g_3=(-2,2).
\]
Here $g_2\perp g_1$, so $m=g_2$ and $h=(1,1)$. Moreover,
$g_3\perp h$, so $\tilde g_3=g_3$. The assembled direction is therefore
$d=(-1,3)$ and $\langle g_1,d\rangle=-1$. For the idealised step
$\theta^+=\theta-sd$, this block contributes
$-s\langle g_1,d\rangle=+s$ to the first-order change in the coarse
objective. It is an increasing contribution, even though both projections have
been applied exactly. Replacing $g_3$ by $(2,-2)$ gives the opposite sign for
$\rho$, confirming that the projection itself imposes no sign constraint.

The remaining scope conditions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Local and block-specific.} The conclusion concerns a
    directional derivative at the current $\theta$. Exact protection of an
    original objective is obtained on a selected two-level block only; on a
    three-level block only the intermediate resultant is protected. Singleton
    blocks are unchanged, and unselected blocks use the unprojected sum. If the
    degeneracy guard fires, the relevant projection is skipped and the
    corresponding orthogonality claim does not apply.
    \item \textbf{The optimiser need not preserve the projected geometry.}
    Momentum mixes the current direction with previous ones, while adaptive
    preconditioning rescales coordinates and need not preserve inner products.
    \Cref{prop:lex_firstorder} therefore constrains the gradient supplied to the
    optimisation rule rather than the realised parameter displacement; regularisation
    may alter that displacement further.
    \item \textbf{Non-differentiable points.} The projection identity
    $\langle\Pi_B(g;r),r\rangle_B=0$ is an algebraic statement about the vectors
    returned by automatic differentiation. It therefore remains true when an
    activation is at a point where the loss has no unique gradient. The Taylor
    expansion at the start of this subsection, however, requires a unique
    gradient. For example, $f(x)=|x|$ is not differentiable at $x=0$; selecting
    the value $g=0$ gives a zero inner product, although any non-zero step changes
    $f$ by an amount proportional to the step size. At such a point the
    projection is still performed as specified, but its inner products cannot be
    interpreted using the differentiable first-order argument.
    \item \textbf{Mini-batch gradients.} Each $g_l$ is computed from the current
    mini-batch rather than from the complete data distribution. The projected
    gradients are therefore orthogonal for that mini-batch only; the
    corresponding full-data gradients need not be orthogonal. The coefficient
    $\gamma$ is also recomputed from each mini-batch and can vary with the
    examples it contains.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Computational cost}
\label{ssec:lex_cost}

The implementation obtains the three level gradients with three calls to
reverse-mode automatic differentiation from one forward graph. A standard
scalarised step uses one backward pass. The graph is retained until the last
level gradient has been computed, and the three gradient tuples coexist until
their projected sum has been assigned to the optimiser. If all losses reach all
$P$ trainable parameters, the three raw gradient tuples alone contain $3P$
entries instead of $P$.
The actual time and peak-memory ratios depend on how much of the model the level
losses share.

Once the gradients are available, a projection on block $P_A$ requires an inner
product, a squared norm and a scaled subtraction for each subordinate direction.
Its arithmetic cost is therefore
\[
O\!\left(\sum_{A} (|A|-1)\,|P_A|\right),
\]
where the sum is over selected blocks. The diagnostic norms and cosines reuse
the same gradients, although their reductions still add computation. In
practice, the extra reverse-mode evaluations and simultaneous storage of the
level gradients are the main overheads.

\section{LH-projection}
\label{sec:methodology_lhproj}

The LH-projection is adapted from the Lexicographic Hybrid Deep Neural Network
of Fiaschi and Cococcioni~\cite{fiaschi2024informed}, which combines lexicographic multi-objective
optimisation with non-standard analysis
\cite{benci2022nonstandard,benci2019measure} and equips a hierarchical classifier
with projection operators before each output layer. Both the LH-projection and lexicographic mode remove from a lower-priority
level's learning signal the part that lies in a subspace already claimed by the
levels above; they differ in where they read that subspace and where they apply
the removal. Lexicographic mode reads it from the higher-priority
\emph{gradients} and removes it from the assembled update
(\S\ref{sec:methodology_lex}); the LH-projection reads it from the higher-priority
\emph{heads} and removes it at the point of the network where those heads pick up
the shared representation.

The generic construction is separated from its Hier-COS instantiation, which
requires changes to the published model. The following sections state the
architectural requirements, define the stop-gradient implementation, describe the
Hier-COS adaptation and delimit the resulting guarantee.

\subsection{Setting and requirements}
\label{ssec:lhproj_setting}

The LH-projection is defined for a particular architectural structure rather than
for a model family. Four conditions are required: the first three make the
operator definable, and the fourth ensures a non-trivial projected subspace.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A single branch point.} There must be one representation
    $z\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ that every level head reads, so that ``the directions the
    higher levels use'' refers to subspaces of one vector space, and so that
    filtering $z$ intercepts the whole of a level's signal to the shared
    parameters.
    \item \textbf{Linear level heads.} The claim the higher levels make on that
    space is identified with the row space of their weight matrices
    $W_1,\dots,W_{l-1}$, corrected by the activation derivative as
    \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_construction} shows. This presupposes that each level score
    is a linear map of $z$; without per-level matrices there is nothing to stack.
    \item \textbf{Branch-local level losses.} Each level term of the objective must
    be a differentiable function of its own head's output alone. If the terms share
    a normaliser, or are otherwise coupled across levels, the level-$l$ signal
    reaches the shared parameters through the other levels' branches as well, and
    filtering what branch $l$ reads no longer determines that signal.
    \item \textbf{A non-trivial complement.} The reserved subspace must not exhaust
    the representation, which requires $D>\sum_{l<L}C_l$. The stacked matrix at the
    finest level has $R_L=\sum_{l<L}C_l$ rows in $\mathbb{R}^{D}$; if
    $R_L\ge D$ and those rows are independent, their span is all of $\mathbb{R}^{D}$,
    the complement is $\{0\}$ and the projection annihilates the entire learning
    signal of the finest level. Since $R_l$ increases with $l$, the binding case is
    $l=L$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Model families satisfying the conditions}

Each model family has to be adapted differently to apply LH-projection:

\emph{LH-DNN} satisfies all four by construction: the branch-point projection is
its own contribution. It is retained as the origin of the mechanism and as a
baseline.

\emph{H-CAST} has no single branch point. Its level heads read different stages of
one staged encoder --- the fine head branches off first and the coarse head reads
the deepest stage --- so the representation a coarse head reads is computed
\emph{from} the representation the fine head already used. That is, the levels reach nested,
not identical, sets of parameters.

\emph{HT-CapsNet} fails the first two conditions together. Its level capsules are
chained, each level receiving the previous level's output capsules among its
inputs, and a level score is the norm of a capsule obtained by routing, not a
linear map of a shared vector.

\emph{HRN} has a shared backbone but cumulative level embeddings: level $l$ reads
the sum of its own projection and those of all coarser levels, so the heads again
do not read one common representation, and its level terms are coupled by the
hierarchical residual construction of the loss.

\emph{Hier-COS} fails conditions~1--3 as published --- it emits a single node
vector, maps it through a dense fixed frame shared by the whole taxonomy, scores
levels by subspace norms (\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}) and trains against a single
node-wide KL term --- but it fails them at points that can be \emph{substituted}
rather than rebuilt: the frame is a fixed matrix that can be chosen differently,
the objective's normaliser can be scoped to a level, and the representation width
$D$ is a free design parameter that condition~4 constrains.

\subsection{The branch projection}
\label{ssec:lhproj_construction}
\label{ssec:lhproj_forward}

Following the construction of LH-DNN~\cite{fiaschi2024informed}, consider a
shared trunk that produces a pre-activation $k_b\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$ and a
representation $z_b=\rho(k_b)$ for sample $b$. Level $l$ has a linear head
$W_l\in\mathbb{R}^{C_l\times D}$. The first level reads $z_b$ directly. For
$l>1$, stack the weights of the higher-priority heads:
\[
W_{<l}:=
\begin{bmatrix}W_1\\ \vdots\\ W_{l-1}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
A^{(l)}[b]
:=W_{<l}\operatorname{diag}\!\big(\rho'(k_b)\big)
\in\mathbb{R}^{R_l\times D},
\qquad
R_l:=\sum_{j<l}C_j.
\]
Writing $A:=A^{(l)}[b]$, the regularised projection matrix associated with the
subspace reserved by the higher-priority heads is
\[
P^{(l)}_\varepsilon[b]
:=A^{\top}\big(AA^{\top}+\varepsilon I\big)^{-1}A,
\qquad
c_b^{(l)}:=P^{(l)}_\varepsilon[b]z_b.
\]
With $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ denoting the identity in value and zero in
derivative, head $l$ receives
\[
z_b^{(l)}:=z_b-c_b^{(l)}+\operatorname{sg}\!\big(c_b^{(l)}\big).
\]
Hence $z_b^{(l)}=z_b$ in the forward pass, while, holding the projection matrix
fixed in the backward pass,
\[
\frac{\partial z_b^{(l)}}{\partial z_b}
=I-P^{(l)}_\varepsilon[b].
\]
The scores produced by a fixed parameter vector are therefore unchanged; only
the level-$l$ gradient sent to the shared trunk is filtered. The higher-head
weights and the activation derivative are detached when the projection matrix is
built, so the level-$l$ loss cannot update them through this auxiliary path.
\Cref{fig:lh_projection_branch} collects the construction.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/methodology/lh_projection_branch.pdf}
\caption{The LH-projection at the branch point, drawn in the convention of
\cite{fiaschi2024informed}. Every level head reads the same representation
$z_b$: the first head reads it unfiltered, while for $l>1$ the projector
$P^{(l)}_{\varepsilon}$ extracts the component $c_b^{(l)}$ lying in the subspace
reserved by the heads above level $l$. The two branches are recombined
additively, but only the perpendicular one, $z_b-c_b^{(l)}$, carries a
derivative: the dotted branch is detached, so $z_b^{(l)}=z_b$ in value while
$\partial z_b^{(l)}/\partial z_b=I-P^{(l)}_{\varepsilon}$ filters the gradient
the level-$l$ loss sends to the shared trunk. The projector is rebuilt for each
sample from the stacked higher-priority weights $W_{<l}$ and the activation
derivative $\rho'(k_b)$, both detached, so the level-$l$ loss cannot reach them
through this path either.}
\label{fig:lh_projection_branch}
\end{figure}

The term $\varepsilon I$ keeps the system solvable when the stacked rows are
linearly dependent or poorly conditioned. For $\varepsilon=0$ and full row rank,
$P^{(l)}_0[b]$ is the orthogonal projector onto
$\operatorname{row}(A^{(l)}[b])$; positive $\varepsilon$ gives a stable but
approximate projection. Because $A^{(l)}[b]$ depends on the sample through
$\rho'(k_b)$, one $R_l\times R_l$ system is solved per sample and projected
level.

\subsection{Hier-COS instantiation}
\label{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}

Hier-COS is the host model for this adaptation because its simple output
path allows the changes required by the LH-projection to be introduced and
evaluated separately. Three changes are structural; each alters the model on
which the projection is subsequently measured.

\subsubsection{(i) Identity frame and per-level heads}
Hier-COS obtains its node coordinates by mapping the transformed backbone feature through a single
$N\times N$ orthonormal matrix --- the \emph{frame} --- drawn at random once
before training and frozen thereafter. The whole output path therefore contains one
linear map, it is shared by every level, and it is not trained. Levels are thus
separated by distinct index sets into one common output vector.

Condition~2 identifies the claim of the levels above $l$ with the row space of their weight matrices
$W_1,\dots,W_{l-1}$, and condition~1 requires those heads to read one common
vector. The published model supplies neither: there is no per-level matrix whose
rows could be stacked.

\paragraph{Modifications.} The adapted model inserts one learnable linear head per level between the branch point
and the frame, so that the node block of level $l$ is produced by its own matrix
$W_l$, and it replaces the dense frame by the identity (equivalently, by a
per-level block-diagonal frame, which leaves the level blocks independent). The
second change is needed because under a dense frame the
coordinates of any level are mixtures of the outputs of \emph{all} heads, so the
map from the shared representation to the scores of level $l$ would involve the
weights of the finer heads as well, and the subspace reserved for the coarse
levels would contain directions owned by the levels the projection is meant to
leave free.

\subsubsection{(ii) Level-scoped normalisation}

Condition~3 requires each level term of the objective to be a differentiable
function of its own head's output alone. That is stronger than requiring $L$
separate numbers, and the gap between the two is what this subsection makes
precise. Write $\mathcal{N}_l\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ for the block of node
coordinates belonging to level $l$: the blocks partition the taxonomy and
$\lvert\mathcal{N}_l\rvert=C_l$. Let $n_l(y)\in\mathcal{N}_l$ be the
ground-truth node of the sample at level $l$, and
$\bar w_l:=w_l/\sum_{l'}w_{l'}$ the normalised level weights of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_objective}. After the substitution of (i) the block of level
$l$ is the output of that level's own head,
\[
u_{\mathcal{N}_l}=W_l\,z_b^{(l)} ,
\]
so asking which coordinates a level term reads is the same as asking which
branches it reaches the shared trunk through.

\paragraph{Three objective forms.} The published objective applies one
softmax over all $N$ taxonomy nodes to the absolute coordinates and matches it
against a target distribution supported on the ground-truth path:
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{KL}}=\operatorname{KL}\!\big(q(y)\,\big\|\,p\big),
\qquad
p_j=\frac{e^{\lvert u_j\rvert}}{\sum_{j'=1}^{N}e^{\lvert u_{j'}\rvert}},
\qquad
q_j(y)=
\begin{cases}
\bar w_l, & j=n_l(y),\\[2pt]
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]
It is a single scalar, and no per-level signal can be extracted from it at all.
The \emph{intermediate} objective keeps that global softmax and reads off the
target node of each level separately, giving one cross-entropy term per level,
\[
\ell^{\mathrm{glob}}_l=-\,\bar w_l\log p_{n_l(y)} .
\]
The \emph{level-scoped} objective instead takes the softmax within the node
block of the level,
\[
\ell^{\mathrm{lvl}}_l=-\,\bar w_l\log p^{(l)}_{n_l(y)},
\qquad
p^{(l)}_j=\frac{e^{\lvert u_j\rvert}}{\sum_{j'\in\mathcal{N}_l}e^{\lvert u_{j'}\rvert}},
\quad j\in\mathcal{N}_l .
\]
All three carry the same level-wise coordinate regulariser,
\[
\mathcal{R}_l=\Big\lVert\,e_{n_l(y)}-\frac{\lvert u_{\mathcal{N}_l}\rvert}{\big\lVert\,\lvert u_{\mathcal{N}_l}\rvert\,\big\rVert_2}\Big\rVert_1 ,
\]
with $e_c$ the standard basis vector of the target node, which reads
$u_{\mathcal{N}_l}$ and nothing else and is therefore separable by construction. The softmax normaliser is thus the only quantity that changes
across the three positions, and the only one that couples the levels.

\paragraph{Exact decomposition under global normalisation.} Summing
the intermediate terms recovers the published objective,
\[
\sum_{l=1}^{L}\ell^{\mathrm{glob}}_l
=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_j(y)\log p_j
=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{KL}}+H\big(q(y)\big),
\qquad
H\big(q(y)\big)=-\sum_{j=1}^{N}q_j(y)\log q_j(y),
\]
and the additive entropy term is a function of the fixed weights alone, not of
$\theta$. The two positions therefore induce identical gradients: the
intermediate objective is the published one re-read as an ordered list of $L$
terms, not a different loss. Nothing about the training problem is traded away
in taking that step, which is why it is not treated as a precondition below.

\paragraph{Effect of level-scoped normalisation.} Under the
global normaliser the derivative of the level-$l$ term does not vanish outside
that level's block:
\[
\frac{\partial \ell^{\mathrm{glob}}_l}{\partial \lvert u_j\rvert}
=\bar w_l\big(p_j-\mathbf{1}[j=n_l(y)]\big)
=\bar w_l\,p_j>0
\qquad\text{for every } j\notin\mathcal{N}_l ,
\]
and hence, through the absolute value, on $u_j$ itself wherever $u_j\neq0$. The
reason is structural rather than incidental: raising the target node's
probability necessarily lowers the mass held everywhere else in the taxonomy,
including at the levels the term is not meant to supervise. Under the
level-scoped normaliser the same derivative is confined to the block,
\[
\frac{\partial \ell^{\mathrm{lvl}}_l}{\partial \lvert u_j\rvert}
=\bar w_l\big(p^{(l)}_j-\mathbf{1}[j=n_l(y)]\big)\,\mathbf{1}\big[j\in\mathcal{N}_l\big] ,
\]
so $\ell^{\mathrm{lvl}}_l$ is a function of $u_{\mathcal{N}_l}$, hence of the
output of head $l$, alone. Condition~3 is met at the third position and not at
the second.

\paragraph{Different requirements of the two gradient mechanisms.}
Lexicographic mode (\S\ref{sec:methodology_lex}) consumes whole
gradients $\nabla_\theta\ell_l$ and needs nothing beyond the terms being
separate and differentiable; by which path a term reached $\theta$ is immaterial
to it, so the intermediate objective already supports it, and the Hier-COS
lexicographic runs use it. The LH-projection asks for more, because it acts on
one specific path. It filters what branch $l$ reads, installing the Jacobian
$I-P^{(l)}_\varepsilon[b]$ there. Under the level-scoped objective that is the
only route the level-$l$ term has to the shared trunk, so the filter determines
the whole of its signal. Under the global normaliser the same term also depends
on $u_{\mathcal{N}_j}=W_j z_b^{(j)}$ for every $j\neq l$, and so reaches the
trunk through those branches as well --- each of them filtered by its own
$P^{(j)}_\varepsilon[b]$, which reserves the levels coarser than $j$ rather than
those coarser than $l$, with the coarsest branch not filtered at all. The
gradient arriving at the shared parameters would then not be the filtered
gradient the guarantee of \S\ref{ssec:lhproj_limits} is stated for, and the
reserved subspace would not be emptied of the lower-priority signal. This is a
property of the normaliser and not of the weighting: no choice of $w$ makes
$\bar w_l\,p_j$ vanish off the block. This is why the projected variant requires
the level-scoped objective and lexicographic runs do not.

\paragraph{Treatment as a structural precondition.} As with the frame, the change
is structural: it alters the model that the projection is subsequently measured
on, so the comparison against the global normaliser quantifies the price of a
requirement rather than the effect of a mechanism. \cref{ch:experiments} reports
it as a two-row contrast at matched frame and weighting, and it also carries the
baseline in both scopes, since comparing a mechanism arm trained under a
per-level normaliser against a baseline trained under a global one would confound
the mechanism with the normaliser. The published KL objective is not one of those
rows: admitting no per-level decomposition, it cannot serve as the baseline for
any arm in this thesis, and it is the starting point of the adaptation rather
than a point of comparison within it. By the identity above this costs nothing,
since the global-normaliser row optimises the same function as the published
objective and therefore stands in for it.

\subsubsection{(iii) A representation width decoupled from the taxonomy}

Condition~4 requires $D>\sum_{l<L}C_l$. In the published model the width is tied
to the taxonomy size $N$, since the node coordinates are what the frame consumes;
the projected variant therefore makes the width of the shared representation a
free parameter.

\paragraph{The activation.} The instantiation places a shared PReLU at the
branch point, so the diagonal entering $A^{(l)}[b]$ is
$\rho'(k)_j=\mathbf{1}[k_j>0]+a_j\,\mathbf{1}[k_j\le 0]$, with $a_j$ the learned
slope of channel $j$, evaluated at the sample's own pre-activation.

\paragraph{Advantage function.} Following \cite{fiaschi2024informed}, the \emph{advantage}
parameterisation is included as an ablation. It expresses the logits of level $l$ as a residual on
top of the parent logit, treated as constant for differentiation,
\[
z_l \leftarrow z_l + z_{l-1}[\pi_{l-1}(\cdot)] ,
\]
so that the head predicts a correction to its parent rather than an absolute
score.

\subsection{Guarantee and scope}
\label{ssec:lhproj_limits}

The guarantee attaching to the LH-projection in \cite{fiaschi2024informed} is
narrower than the construction may suggest, and three qualifications should be
carried into the interpretation of the results. They parallel those of
\S\ref{ssec:lex_guarantee}.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-order.} The statement concerns the gradient at the current
    parameters. Before numerical regularisation is considered, the filtered
    level-$l$ gradient with respect to $z_b$ has no component along the reserved
    subspace. This local statement does not
    guarantee that the higher-priority objectives do not increase after a finite
    optimiser step, and says nothing about the limit of the training trajectory.
    \item \textbf{Per-sample and branch-point-local.} The projection is applied at
    the branch point, on the representation each head reads. Interactions between
    level objectives that are mediated by parameters upstream of the branch point,
    or by batch statistics such as those of the normalisation layers, are outside
    its scope.
    \item \textbf{Numerically regularised.} With $\varepsilon>0$,
    $P^{(l)}_\varepsilon[b]$ is a regularised approximation rather than an exact
    orthogonal projector. If $\sigma_i$ is a singular value of $A^{(l)}[b]$, the
    fraction left along its corresponding reserved direction is
    $\varepsilon/(\sigma_i^2+\varepsilon)$. The removal therefore becomes less
    exact as $\sigma_i$ approaches zero.
\end{enumerate}
\section{Supervision of taxonomy-subspace scores}
\label{sec:methodology_subspace}
\label{ssec:methodology_subspace_ce}

Direct subspace supervision acts on the \emph{objective}. The preceding mechanisms
leave the loss unchanged and modify its gradient signal; this mechanism leaves
the signal path unchanged and modifies the quantity supplied to the loss. The
native Hier-COS objective supervises the taxonomy-node coordinates $u$: it
constructs a sparse probability distribution supported on the ground-truth path,
applies a global softmax to $\lvert u\rvert$, and combines the resulting KL term
with a level-wise coordinate regulariser. The subspace norms $z_l$ of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores} are then used as the readout at inference, so what is
trained and what is ranked are two different functions of the same coordinates.

The direct-subspace objective supervises the readout itself. This requires a
target for each subspace norm. These scores are coupled: the subspace of a node
contains its ancestors, itself and its descendants, and therefore overlaps the
subspaces of related classes. A one-hot target would not describe the intended
path representation. In particular, the model could approach such a target by
concentrating its energy in the ground-truth leaf coordinate and leaving the
ancestor coordinates inactive. The target used here is instead obtained by
applying the subspace decoder itself to a canonical representation of the
ground-truth path, as derived in \S\ref{ssec:subspace_objective}.

\subsection{Setting and requirements}
\label{ssec:subspace_setting}

The mechanism requires a network that exposes, for every level, the vector of
taxonomy-subspace scores that its own decoder consumes. All the families
compared here have the possibility to do so, and the mechanism is therefore
applicable to all of them.

Two further restrictions are part of the condition. First, the objective is a deterministic function of the hard
ground-truth path, so the arm uses neither label smoothing nor mixup or cutmix.
The target constructed below distributes mass over several classes, but it does
so as a function of the taxonomy alone; an input-mixing scheme would additionally
make the \emph{path} a mixture, and the comparison against the native objective
would no longer isolate the readout. Second, this mechanism is not combined with
any of the other three. Against the LH-projection the exclusion is structural,
as \S\ref{ssec:design_axis} sets out; against HCC and lexicographic mode it keeps
the intervention attributable.

\subsection{The target}
\label{ssec:subspace_objective}

As in \S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}, $I_{l,c}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ contains
the coordinate indices assigned to class $c$ at level $l$: its ancestors,
itself and its descendants. Let $J(y)\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ be the set of
coordinates on the ground-truth path.

Rather than defining a label independently of the decoder, the construction
starts from a canonical coordinate representation of this path. It distributes
unit energy uniformly over its $L$ nodes:
\[
u_j^*(y):=
\begin{cases}
1/\sqrt{L}, & j\in J(y),\\
0, & j\notin J(y).
\end{cases}
\]
The target is the result of applying the same taxonomy-subspace score used at
inference to $u^*(y)$. Write $z_l[c;v]$ for the score of class $c$ evaluated at
coordinate vector $v$. If
\[
r_l(c):=\bigl\lvert I_{l,c}\cap J(y)\bigr\rvert
\]
counts how many ground-truth path coordinates fall in the subspace of class
$c$, then
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:subspace_target}
t_l[c]
:=z_l[c;u^*(y)]
=\left(\sum_{j\in I_{l,c}}(u_j^*(y))^2\right)^{1/2}
=\left(\frac{r_l(c)}{L}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
For the correct class, $r_l(y_l)=L$ because its subspace contains the complete
path, and hence $t_l[y_l]=1$. An incorrect class contains only those
ground-truth coordinates shared down to its lowest common ancestor with $y_l$,
so $r_l(c)\leq l-1$ and its target decreases with the amount of shared path.
The full target profile is therefore not an ad hoc smoothing of a class label:
it is exactly the decoder output induced by the reference path geometry. The
signs of the non-zero coordinates are immaterial because the readout uses their
squared values.

This derivation also explains why supervising only $z_l[y_l]$, or replacing
$t_l$ with a one-hot vector, is insufficient. The correct score alone does not
constrain the relative scores of competing subspaces or determine how energy is
distributed among the nodes on the path. A one-hot objective can favour a
leaf-only solution, which separates the classes while discarding the ancestor
coordinates that the path representation is meant to retain. Conversely, using
the model's current coordinates restricted to $J(y)$ as the target would make
the target depend on the prediction and would preserve whichever path-energy
allocation the model already produced. The fixed vector $u^*(y)$ supplies a
taxonomy-defined, attainable reference instead.

\subsection{The loss}
\label{ssec:subspace_loss}

For a non-zero coordinate vector $u$, define the normalised scores
\[
\bar z_l:=\frac{z_l}{\lVert u\rVert_2}.
\]
The same scale is used at every level. The implementation clips the denominator
by a small positive constant when $\lVert u\rVert_2$ is close to zero.

The target and prediction distributions are obtained with the same temperature
$\tau>0$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
q_l&:=\operatorname{softmax}(t_l/\tau),
\qquad
p_l:=\operatorname{softmax}(\bar z_l/\tau),\\[0.4ex]
\ell_l^{\mathrm{sub}}&:=-\sum_{c=1}^{C_l}q_l[c]\log p_l[c],
\end{aligned}
\]
and the complete objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:subspace_loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sub}}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_l\,\ell_l^{\mathrm{sub}},
\qquad \textstyle\sum_l w_l=1,
\end{equation}
using the same leaf-heavy weights as the Hier-COS baseline.

The reference vector $u^*(y)$ has unit norm and, by construction, its subspace
scores are $t_l$. Normalisation therefore leaves those scores unchanged, so the
reference geometry gives $\bar z_l=t_l$ and hence $p_l=q_l$. The soft
cross-entropy can be written as
\[
\ell_l^{\mathrm{sub}}
=H(q_l)+\operatorname{KL}(q_l\Vert p_l),
\]
where $H(q_l)$ is fixed with respect to the model parameters. It is the
unavoidable value of the loss when the target itself is soft, rather than a
remaining prediction error. The part that training can reduce is
$\operatorname{KL}(q_l\Vert p_l)$, which is non-negative and vanishes when the
predicted distribution matches the target distribution. Thus the minimum loss
is generally $H(q_l)$, not zero.

The comparison is between the two distributions, rather than a component-wise
equality constraint on the raw score vectors; in particular, softmax is
unchanged by adding the same constant to every entry. The temperature sets the
resolution of this comparison: a smaller $\tau$ concentrates probability on
the largest scores and emphasises their gaps, whereas a larger $\tau$ produces
a softer target and prediction profile.

For a non-zero subspace norm, the coordinate derivative of the score is
\[
\frac{\partial z_l[c]}{\partial u_j}
=
\begin{cases}
u_j/z_l[c], & j\in I_{l,c},\\
0, & j\notin I_{l,c},
\end{cases}
\]
and at an exactly zero norm the implementation selects the finite zero
subgradient. The loss therefore backpropagates through the same coordinates used
to compute the Hier-COS readout.

\subsection{The attainable margin}
\label{ssec:subspace_margin}

For an incorrect class at level $l$, $r_l(c)\leq l-1$. Substituting this bound
in \eqref{eq:subspace_target} gives the smallest possible target gap between the
correct class and its closest competitor.

\begin{proposition}[Attainable margin]
\label{prop:subspace_margin}
Let $t_l$ be the target in \eqref{eq:subspace_target}. For every
$c\neq y_l$,
\[
t_l[y_l]-t_l[c]\;\ge\;\gamma_l:=1-\Big(\frac{l-1}{L}\Big)^{1/2},
\]
with equality when $c$ is a sibling of $y_l$; at level $1$, equality holds for
every incorrect class.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The correct class has $r_l(y_l)=L$. An incorrect class shares at most the first
$l-1$ path coordinates, with equality for a sibling. The result follows from
\eqref{eq:subspace_target}.
\end{proof}

The bound decreases with depth. For the three-level taxonomies used in this
study, it is $1.000$, $0.423$ and $0.184$ from coarse to fine. The temperature $\tau$
rescales them inside the softmax, and its effect is evaluated empirically.

\subsection{Guarantee and scope}
This mechanism provides no ordering or path-consistency guarantee. Its narrower
purpose is to train the same score vector that the decoder ranks, using a target
that can be produced by the subspace readout. The following limitations apply.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The level losses are combined by the weighted sum in
    \eqref{eq:subspace_loss}. No level has lexicographic priority, and independent
    decoding can still produce an inconsistent path.
    \item The target assumes an equal energy split over the ground-truth path.
    This is a modelling choice. Its
    depth-dependent separation is given by \cref{prop:subspace_margin}.
    \item The reference profile is reachable, but this does not imply a unique
    coordinate vector or guarantee that optimisation will recover the reference
    geometry. The loss compares the distributions $q_l$ and $p_l$.
    \item A coordinate that is exactly zero receives a zero gradient through the
    subspace norm. In addition, away from the numerical clipping near zero,
    normalisation by $\lVert u\rVert_2$ makes the loss invariant to a positive
    rescaling of $u$.
    \item The mechanism is inactive at inference. Any measured difference comes
    from the parameters learned during training; the readout and decoders remain
    those of \S\ref{sec:methodology_inference}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Hierarchical Constraint Cascade}
\label{sec:methodology_hcc}

The \emph{Hierarchical Constraint Cascade} (HCC) acts on the outputs, enforcing
hierarchical relations among the scores of a multi-level
classifier by a recursive affine correction of the model outputs. The method
builds on the \textsc{HardNet} framework for imposing hard constraints on neural
networks \cite{min2024hardnet}, of which it is the equality-only case applied
recursively along a taxonomy.

\subsection{Setting and requirements}
\label{ssec:hcc_setting}

HCC is defined on the level-wise scores of the classifier rather than on its
internal structure. Three conditions are required:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item a vector of differentiable scores for each level of the hierarchy;
    \item a complete taxonomy, with a unique parent for every non-root class and
    no parent class without children (the condition required by
    \cref{lemma:diag});
    \item a consistent ordering of the levels from coarsest to finest.
\end{enumerate}

The same cascade can therefore be attached to models with transformer,
convolutional, capsule-based or taxonomic-frame backbones, provided their outputs
can be expressed in this common form, and it supports an arbitrary number of
levels by repetition of the same operation. This concerns applicability only: the
effect of the constraint, and its interaction with the previous-level detach introduced
below, must be verified experimentally for each combination of model, dataset and
decoding strategy.

The mathematical construction is stated for arbitrary $L$, but every taxonomy
in the experimental campaign has three levels and the shared implementation is
specialised to exactly three. Thus the experiments evaluate the two-transition
cascade $1\to2\to3$; they do not provide empirical evidence for longer
compositions.

\subsubsection{The case for a hard constraint}

Input--output relations are commonly introduced either as penalties, which do
not guarantee satisfaction, or through specialised parameterisations that may
reduce representational capacity~\cite{min2024hardnet}. HardNet constructs
networks that satisfy input-dependent hard constraints while preserving
unconstrained parameter optimisation and universal approximation. HardNet-Aff is
used here because its affine constraints admit a closed-form transformation,
unlike iterative correction procedures such as DC3.

\subsubsection{The HardNet-Aff projection}

Let
$f_\theta:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}$ be a network. HardNet
enforces the input-dependent affine constraint
$b^l(x)\le A(x)f(x)\le b^u(x)$, with $A(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_c\times
n_{\mathrm{out}}}$, through a differentiable projection. Under
feasibility and full row rank of $A(x)$, the closed-form solution is
\cite{min2024hardnet}
\[
\resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{P}(f_\theta)(x)
=
f_\theta(x)
+
A(x)^{+}
\Big[
\operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(b^l(x)-A(x)f_\theta(x)\big)
-\operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(A(x)f_\theta(x)-b^u(x)\big)
\Big],
$}
\]
The matrix
\[
A(x)^{+}:=A(x)^{\top}\big(A(x)A(x)^{\top}\big)^{-1}
\]
is the Moore--Penrose pseudoinverse. Two properties established in
\cite{min2024hardnet} are used here:
the projected output satisfies each violated constraint exactly and leaves the
others unchanged (Proposition 4.4); and if $f_\theta$ universally approximates a
class $\mathcal{F}$, the projected class universally approximates the subset of
$\mathcal{F}$ satisfying the constraint (Theorem 4.6), so enforcing the constraint
does not, in the limit, reduce representational capacity.

The notation of \S\ref{sec:methodology_problem} is used throughout: $z_l$ denotes
the level-$l$ scores, levels are ordered coarse to fine, and
$M_l\in\{0,1\}^{C_l\times C_{l+1}}$ is the incidence matrix of the
parent--child relation at the transition $l\to l+1$, with exactly one non-zero
entry per column. Only the two
structural assumptions of \S\ref{ssec:problem_taxonomy} are required by the
derivation that follows.

\subsection{The hierarchical cascade}
\label{ssec:hardnet_to_hcc}
\label{ssec:hcc_extensions}

HCC instantiates HardNet-Aff separately for each level transition $l\to l+1$,
setting $A(x)\equiv M_l$ --- a \emph{fixed} matrix, not input-dependent,
since it encodes the structure of the taxonomy rather than the input --- and
imposing an equality-only constraint, $b^l=b^u=\hat z_l$:
\[
M_l\hat z_{l+1}=\hat z_l .
\]
An equality constraint is the degenerate case of the box constraint obtained by
setting $b^l=b^u$ (Remark 4.1 in \cite{min2024hardnet}). The constraint is imposed
in \emph{logit space}, and in general it does not imply
$\operatorname{softmax}(\hat z_l)=M_l\operatorname{softmax}(\hat z_{l+1})$.
HCC must therefore not be read as a normalisation of probability mass between
parent and children, but as a structural affine constraint on the representations
fed to the objective.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:hardnet_equality}
Let $b^{l}=b^{u}=b$. Then the two rectifiers of the HardNet-Aff projection collapse
into a single affine correction,
\[
\mathcal{P}(f_\theta)(x)
=
f_\theta(x)-A(x)^{+}\big(A(x)f_\theta(x)-b(x)\big),
\]
which is applied at every step, whether or not the constraint was violated.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For every $v\in\mathbb{R}$,
\[
\operatorname{ReLU}(v)-\operatorname{ReLU}(-v)
=
\begin{cases}
v-0=v, & v\ge 0,\\[0.4ex]
0-(-v)=v, & v<0,
\end{cases}
\]
and the identity extends componentwise to vectors. Setting
$v:=b-A(x)f_\theta(x)$ in the general HardNet-Aff expression with
$b^{l}=b^{u}=b$ therefore gives
\[
\operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(b-Af_\theta\big)-\operatorname{ReLU}\!\big(Af_\theta-b\big)
=
b-Af_\theta ,
\]
so that
$\mathcal{P}(f_\theta)(x)=f_\theta(x)+A^{+}\big(b-Af_\theta(x)\big)$, which is the
claim. Since no rectifier remains, the correction no longer vanishes on the
feasible side of the constraint.
\end{proof}

Applying this with $A=M_l$, $f_\theta(x)=z_{l+1}$ and $b=\hat z_l$ recovers
the Euclidean projection onto the affine subspace $\{q:M_lq=\hat z_l\}$:
\[
\hat z_{l+1}
=
z_{l+1}
-
M_l^{\top}
\left(
M_lM_l^{\top}
\right)^{-1}
\left(
M_lz_{l+1}-\hat z_l
\right).
\]
By Proposition 4.4 in \cite{min2024hardnet}, specialised to $b^l=b^u$, this
projection satisfies the constraint \emph{exactly} for any $z_{l+1}$, whether or
not the constraint was initially violated.

\subsubsection{A structural property specific to taxonomies}

In the general case
$A(x)$ may vary with the input and the invertibility of $A(x)A(x)^{\top}$ must be
verified pointwise. In HCC the matrix is fixed, and its single-parent structure
admits a closed form.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:diag}
If every column of $M_l$ contains exactly one non-zero entry, then $M_lM_l^{\top}$ is diagonal, with $i$-th diagonal entry equal to the number of child classes of class $i$ at level $l$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write $M:=M_l$. For $i=i'$ we have $(MM^\top)_{i,i}=\sum_j M[i,j]^2=\sum_j M[i,j]$, since $M$ is binary, which equals the number $n_i$ of children of class $i$. For $i\ne i'$ we have $(MM^\top)_{i,i'}=\sum_j M[i,j]M[i',j]$; by hypothesis, for each column $j$ at most one of $M[i,j]$ and $M[i',j]$ is non-zero, so every summand vanishes and the entry is zero.
\end{proof}

It follows that $M_lM_l^{\top}=\operatorname{diag}(n_1,\dots,n_{C_l})$,
which is invertible if and only if every class at level $l$ has at least one child
--- a structural condition that can be verified once for the taxonomy rather
than for every $x$. The regularised term
$M_lM_l^{\top}+\varepsilon I$ stays diagonal, so the linear system
is diagonal and costs $O(C_l)$ per sample.

\subsubsection{Recursive cascade}

Setting $\hat z_1=z_1$ and applying the projection
recursively, with the stop-gradient operator $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ on the
right-hand side, yields

\begin{align*}
\hat z_1 &= z_1,\\
\hat z_2 &= \mathcal{P}_{1,2}\big(z_2;\operatorname{sg}(\hat z_1)\big),\\
&\;\vdots\\
\hat z_L &= \mathcal{P}_{L-1,L}\big(z_L;\operatorname{sg}(\hat z_{L-1})\big),
\end{align*}

where each $\mathcal{P}_{l,l+1}$ is an instance of equality-only HardNet-Aff applied to the
level pair $(l,l+1)$. The coarsest level is left unchanged and acts as the
reference; each subsequent level is projected onto the affine set determined by
the level above it, producing a coarse-to-fine cascade.

\medskip

The recursive construction introduces two choices not present in HardNet-Aff.

\subsubsection{Previous-level detach}

In the original formulation $b(x)$ does not
depend on $\theta$, so derivative coupling between $b$ and the projection does not
arise. In HCC, $\hat z_l$ is itself differentiable with respect to $\theta$.
Without further provision, the derivative of the level-$(l+1)$ objective would
also modify $\hat z_l$ through the projection, mixing the two objectives along
that path. The operator $\operatorname{sg}(\cdot)$ treats $\hat z_l$ as fixed for
this derivative while preserving its value. This is what is called the
\emph{previous-level detach} throughout: the finer level is projected onto the
constraint set determined by the level above it, while the level above is detached
from the backward pass of that projection. Influence is consequently directed
from coarse to fine in both the score transformation and optimisation. The
stabilised form used throughout is therefore
\[
\hat z_{l+1}
=
z_{l+1}
-
M_l^{\top}
\left(
M_lM_l^{\top}
+
\varepsilon I
\right)^{-1}
\left(
M_lz_{l+1}-\operatorname{sg}(\hat z_l)
\right).
\]
For $\varepsilon=0$ and full row rank the constraints hold exactly; for
$\varepsilon>0$ they hold up to the numerical precision set by the stabilisation
term. \Cref{fig:hcc_cascade} shows the cascade and the placement of the detach.

\subsubsection{Composition over several transitions}

HardNet-Aff is applied and
evaluated as a single terminal layer in \cite{min2024hardnet}, whereas HCC applies
it recursively across $L-1$ successive transitions. The guarantees of exact
satisfaction (Proposition 4.4) and preservation of universal approximation
(Theorem 4.6) concern a single application; their extension by induction over
levels to the full cascade with $L>2$ is not proved in this work and remains an
open question. Exact satisfaction between two consecutive levels is guaranteed by
each projection taken individually, while the effect of composing the projections
with the previous-level detach on the overall representational capacity must be assessed
empirically.

\subsubsection{The training objective}

HCC introduces no new loss: it changes the arguments on
which the existing level objectives are evaluated,
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{HCC}}
=
\sum_{l=1}^{L}
w_l\,\ell_l\big(\hat z_l,y_l\big)
+
\lambda\,\mathcal{R}\big(\hat z_1,\dots,\hat z_L\big),
\]
with $w_l$ the coefficients of \S\ref{ssec:problem_objective}. Because the
projection is differentiable, the model is not merely corrected after the fact: it
is trained on the corrected scores from the first step, and adapts its raw scores
to the correction that will be applied to them.

\subsection{Adaptation by model family}
\label{ssec:hcc_family_adaptation}

The affine operator is shared across families, but the phrase ``level-wise
scores'' does not denote the same internal quantity in every architecture. The
tensor to which HCC is attached, and the transformation between that tensor and
the native loss or readout, must therefore be fixed before a family-level result
can be interpreted. In every trained HCC arm the correction is binary and is
applied from the first optimisation step; there is no onset epoch or interpolation
coefficient.

\paragraph{H-CAST.} HCC receives the three classifier-head logits in
coarse-to-fine order. The corrected logits replace the raw logits as the score
source for the level-wise cross-entropies and, when enabled, the global KL term.
They are also the scores exposed to the standard evaluation path. This is the
direct instantiation of the notation $z_l\mapsto\hat z_l$ used in the derivation
above: no family-specific activation or readout lies between the projection and
the level loss.

\paragraph{Hier-COS.} Hier-COS does not natively classify with its signed node
coordinates. It takes their magnitudes in the objective and aggregates them into
ancestor--self--descendant subspace norms at inference. HCC is inserted before
both operations: the fixed-frame node-coordinate vector is split into its
contiguous level blocks, those signed blocks are projected, and the corrected
blocks are concatenated before the Hier-COS objective and subspace readout are
evaluated. HCC changes the coordinates those two operations receive; it does not
change their form. The affine constraint is therefore imposed on the signed node
coordinates, not in general on their absolute values, subspace norms or
softmax probabilities. In particular, the sibling-order invariance of
\cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift} applies directly to the coordinates but need not
survive a magnitude-based readout. The experimental HCC arm disables both the
optional LH-style branch projection and direct subspace supervision, in
accordance with the single-mechanism design of \S\ref{ssec:design_axis}. It is
trained on the decomposed \texttt{global\_softmax\_ce\_reg} objective at
\texttt{kl\_leaf} level weights rather than on the published node-wide
\texttt{kl\_reg} term, and its baseline row uses the same objective and the same
weights, so that the reported difference isolates the constraint and not the
decomposition.

\paragraph{HT-CapsNet.} The projected quantities are the scalar capsule-length
scores produced after routing at the three stages, not the capsule vectors
themselves. The resulting affine-corrected values are used by the three margin
losses and by evaluation. Once corrected, they are algebraic class scores and
need not themselves be Euclidean lengths; HCC consequently constrains the
length-derived output interface without altering routing or the internal capsule
representations.

\paragraph{HRN.} HRN is the only family studied here whose fine-level decision is
read from a different tensor than the one its hierarchical objective consumes.
Its tree branch emits a pre-sigmoid logit per node at all three levels and drives
the objective of \S\ref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}, while a separate auxiliary
softmax classifier supplies the leaf cross-entropy and the model's reported fine
prediction. The constrained triple therefore has to be chosen, and it is the
triple the model decodes: the coarse and middle tree logits together with the
auxiliary leaf logits.

The corrected values re-enter the model at both points where they are used. The
coarsest level is the anchor and is returned unchanged. The corrected middle
logits pass through the sigmoid and enter the tree objective in place of the raw
ones, and the corrected leaf logits are used by the auxiliary cross-entropy and
by evaluation. The fine head of the tree branch is not part of the constrained
triple and keeps its raw logits. No loss mode is required: because the level
terms telescope to the paper-aligned objective
(\S\ref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}), the conditional split and the native mode
optimise the same quantity, and the arm remains comparable to a native baseline
under either.

Constraining the tree branch's fine head instead was rejected on two grounds.
First, it would leave the reported prediction untouched, because the model
decodes the auxiliary classifier; the arm would forfeit exactly the property
that separates HCC from the gradient-space mechanisms
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_interaction}). Second, the tree logits sit far from the
constraint. Trained through the sigmoid, they carry a large negative offset
shared by nearly every node, so a parent's children sum to a value nowhere near
it. Measured on the unconstrained baseline checkpoints of the three datasets,
the projection would move each fine tree logit by $1.9$ to $2.6$ times that
head's own standard deviation, against $0.4$ to $0.6$ for the auxiliary
classifier; the same figure for H-CAST, whose scores the cascade constrains
natively, is about $1.0$.

Two qualifications remain. The constraint relates a softmax logit to two
pre-sigmoid tree logits, which are not scores of the same kind: the former is
defined only up to a per-sample additive constant, the latter are not. What the
correction does to the auxiliary classifier is nevertheless the behaviour the
cascade is defined by, since it adds a common scalar within each sibling group
(\cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}) and therefore re-ranks classes across sibling
groups while preserving the order inside each one. The coarse-to-middle
transition, common to either choice, is also a larger intervention here than
elsewhere: about $1.0$ to $1.5$ times the middle head's standard deviation,
against $0.3$ to $0.7$ for H-CAST. Of the families studied, HRN is the one whose
emitted scores sit furthest from the cascade's constraint.

Because the constrained triple is the emitted one, the post-hoc projection of
\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid} reconstructs the training-time correction exactly,
as it does for the other families.

\paragraph{LH-DNN.} No trained HCC arm is attached to LH-DNN. Its output
structure is retained as the baseline expression of its built-in branch
projection, and replacing those outputs during training would change the object
the baseline is intended to evaluate. This exclusion does not prevent the
parameter-free HCC transform from being applied post hoc to a frozen LH-DNN
checkpoint: the inference-only analysis needs only its three emitted score
vectors. A post-hoc LH-DNN row therefore measures an output correction and must
not be read as evidence from an LH-DNN model trained under HCC.

\subsection{Guarantee and scope}
\label{ssec:hcc_guarantee}

The cascade realises in output space the level-set restriction of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_priority}. The projection can be written as
\[
\hat z_{l+1}
=
\arg\min_{q\in\mathbb{R}^{C_{l+1}}}
\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert q-z_{l+1}\rVert_2^2
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
M_lq=\hat z_l ,
\]
so the coarse level contributes an equality constraint --- a level set --- inside
which the fine level is resolved in the minimum-perturbation sense. Three
properties follow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dominance is one-directional: the coarse level determines
    the feasible set for the fine level, and the previous-level detach extends that
    asymmetry to the gradient.
    \item The constraint is hard: being an equality
    enforced exactly, coarse-level consistency is not traded against fine-level
    accuracy at any finite value of the coefficients $w_l$.
    \item  And the restriction is
    available in closed form by \cref{lemma:diag}, at the cost of a diagonal
    rescaling, where a lexicographic optimisation procedure would solve a constrained
    subproblem.
\end{itemize}

Two differences from lexicographic optimisation are substantive. First,
lexicographic optimisation prioritises \emph{objectives} and requires the
higher-priority problem to be solved to optimality before the next is considered.
HCC constrains \emph{outputs}, and its anchor $\hat z_1=z_1$ is the network's
current coarse prediction, not the coarse-optimal one; all levels continue to
train jointly. As with the two gradient-space mechanisms, this is lexicographic
\emph{structure} without lexicographic \emph{sequential optimality}. Second, the
protection afforded by the previous-level detach is partial: it removes the influence of
the fine-level loss on the coarse scores along the projection branch, but the
level losses still interact through the shared backbone, so parameter-space
priority is not enforced.

The projection also has a simple consequence for the corrected scores.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}
The HCC correction adds to every child of a fixed parent the same scalar. It
therefore never reorders two classes sharing a parent, and whenever predictions
are read off the corrected scores directly, any change it produces in an
independently decoded prediction --- and hence in
$\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{independent}}$ --- arises exclusively from the reordering of
classes belonging to \emph{different} parents.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By \cref{lemma:diag} the matrix inverted in the correction is diagonal, so the
vector $M^{\top}(MM^{\top}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}(Mz_{l+1}-\hat z_l)$ has, in the
coordinate of child $j$, the value of the coefficient of its parent $\pi_l(j)$
alone. Two children of the same parent therefore receive an identical additive
term, which cancels in their difference and preserves their relative order.
Independent decoding is an unrestricted argmax over the whole level, so the only
comparisons whose outcome the correction can alter are those between classes with
distinct parents, whose additive terms differ.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{Gradient attenuation}
\label{ssec:hcc_gradient_cost}

Backpropagation through the projection can weaken the learning signal received by
the finer head. Appendix~C of \cite{min2024hardnet} notes that an enforcement layer
may produce a vanishing gradient when the output-loss gradient points along the
active constraint directions. This is especially relevant to HCC because its
equality constraints are always active.

In the taxonomic case, an exact projection removes the part of the finer-level
signal that would raise or lower all children of one parent together; numerical
stabilisation attenuates the same component. Those common shifts are fixed by the
parent-level constraint, while the finer head can still learn differences among
siblings. The gradient can therefore become small, or vanish in the exact case,
when much of the original signal lies in the filtered directions. This risk is
greater at transitions with few children per parent, where fewer within-sibling
directions remain available. Whether it harms training depends on how much useful
fine-level information lies in those directions and must be assessed empirically.

Finally, the cascade constrains consistency in logit space and does not by itself
deliver consistent predictions; \S\ref{ssec:inference_interaction} states what it
does and does not guarantee once a decoder is applied.

\section{Hierarchical inference strategies}
\label{sec:methodology_inference}

The preceding sections described how a network is trained. Turning its scores
into a predicted path is a separate methodological decision: the same trained
parameter state read out and decoded in two different ways yields different
accuracies, a different full-path accuracy and, in particular, a different
measured inconsistency.

The decision factorises into two independent choices, and the section is
organised accordingly:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \textbf{readout} $u\mapsto(z_1,\dots,z_L)$, which reduces the
    network's internal taxonomy-node coordinates to one score vector per level
    (\S\ref{ssec:inference_readout});
    \item a \textbf{decoder} $(z_1,\dots,z_L)\mapsto\hat y$, which turns level
    scores into a predicted path (\S\ref{ssec:inference_decoding}).
\end{enumerate}

Both are properties of evaluation rather than of the trained model. They can
therefore be varied while the parameter state is held fixed, which makes the grid
of \S\ref{ssec:inference_grid} an analytical instrument rather than a convention.
The section then states the model-selection rule that keeps decoder comparisons
valid (\S\ref{ssec:inference_selection}), and closes by recording which of the
training-time mechanisms can act at inference at all
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_interaction}). The metrics computed for a chosen cell of
the grid are defined with the experimental setup, in
\cref{ssec:inference_metrics}, and are referred to here by name.

\subsection{Readout rules}
\label{ssec:inference_readout}

Every family considered here can be viewed as emitting a vector of
\emph{node coordinates} $u\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ over the taxonomy, partitioned into
per-level blocks. For the classifier-head families (H-CAST, HRN, HT-CapsNet,
LH-DNN) the blocks are simply the per-level logits of
\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}; for Hier-COS they are the coordinates of the frame
output. Two readouts are defined on this common representation.

\subsubsection{Node score}

Each node is ranked by its own coordinate,
\[
z_l[c] \;=\; \phi\big(u[c]\big),
\]
with $\phi$ the identity for signed classifier logits and $\phi=\lvert\cdot\rvert$
for Hier-COS frame coordinates. The distinction is due to the fact that Hier-COS trains its node distribution as
$\operatorname{log\,softmax}(\lvert u\rvert)$, so its objective never constrains
the sign of a coordinate and only the magnitude carries class evidence. In both
cases the rule ranks nodes by the quantity that the model's own objective drives
up for the correct node.

\subsubsection{Subspace norm}

Each node is ranked by the joint magnitude of the taxonomy
coordinates assigned to it, comprising its ancestors, itself and its
descendants:
\[
z_l[c] \;=\; \Big(\sum_{j\in I_{l,c}}u_j^2\Big)^{1/2} .
\]
This is the native readout of Hier-COS (\S\ref{ssec:problem_scores}), and one
observation about that construction is what makes it available to every other
family without touching the model.

\paragraph{Subspace norm is applicable to every family.} Hier-COS maps the
backbone feature through a fixed orthonormal frame; the taxonomy determines which
axes span each node's subspace \cite{sani2025hiercos}. Because the identity is an
admissible frame --- and the one required by the projected variant of
\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(i) --- classifier-head models already have the required
form: concatenating their per-level score vectors yields $N$ node coordinates in
the identity frame. The subspace norm can therefore be applied at inference to
every family without adding parameters or recomputing coordinates. Only the rule
that assembles each node's score changes, using the same taxonomy masks as in
Hier-COS.

\paragraph{Requirements of the subspace norm.} The norm requires coordinates
whose magnitudes have a consistent interpretation. Squaring removes their signs,
and the result is not invariant to monotone reparameterisation:
$\lVert f(u)\rVert$ is not generally a monotone function of $\lVert u\rVert$.
Thus, ranking nodes correctly is not sufficient; training must make coordinate
magnitude represent evidence. How this condition is met differs across families.

Hier-COS satisfies the condition natively: it trains its node distribution as
$\operatorname{log\,softmax}(\lvert u\rvert)$ with a level-wise regulariser that
drives the normalised magnitudes of each level block towards a one-hot, so a
coordinate's magnitude \emph{is} the evidence for its node and the non-target
magnitudes are already near zero. Its raw coordinates are aggregated unchanged.
Classifier logits carry no such calibration, and fail in two different ways. A
softmax logit is shift-invariant, so its magnitude is arbitrary and only
within-level differences are determined. A sigmoid/BCE logit is worse: the
objective drives a non-target logit to a large negative value, so squaring it reads
a confident rejection as strong support and can invert the ranking outright. For
the classifier-head families the coordinates are therefore mapped through each
model's own output activation --- the quantity the model itself treats as evidence
--- before the norm is taken: a per-level softmax for H-CAST, LH-DNN and
HT-CapsNet, and, for HRN, a sigmoid at its two tree levels and a softmax at its
leaf cross-entropy head. This is the only modification the readout requires
anywhere; it adds no parameter and no tuning decision, since the activation is
fixed by the family's own loss.

\subsection{Decoding rules}
\label{ssec:inference_decoding}

\subsubsection{Independent decoding}

Each level is decoded on its own,
\[
\hat y_l=\arg\max_{c}\; z_l[c],\qquad l=1,\dots,L ,
\]
using no structural information, so the resulting path need not be consistent.

This is the rule the compared families report under. Their published metrics are
per-level and none of them restricts a level by the prediction of the level above:
H-CAST~\cite{park2024visually}, HRN~\cite{chen2022label},
HT-CapsNet~\cite{noor2025taxonomy} and LH-DNN~\cite{fiaschi2024informed} read an
unrestricted argmax at each level, and the reference Hier-COS
implementation~\cite{sani2025hiercos} likewise ranks each level by its
subspace-projection norms without masking.

\subsubsection{Top-down decoding}

The coarsest level is decoded first and each
subsequent level is restricted to the children of the level above:
\[
\hat y_1=\arg\max_{c}\;z_1[c],
\qquad
\hat y_{l}=\arg\max_{c\,:\,\pi_{l-1}(c)=\hat y_{l-1}}\; z_{l}[c],
\quad l=2,\dots,L .
\]

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:topdown_consistency}
If the taxonomy satisfies the ``no childless parent'' assumption, the path
produced by top-down decoding is consistent for every input.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By induction. $\hat y_1$ is unconstrained. For $l>1$, the feasible set
$\{c:\pi_{l-1}(c)=\hat y_{l-1}\}$ is non-empty by assumption, so the masked argmax
is attained on it and $\pi_{l-1}(\hat y_l)=\hat y_{l-1}$ holds by construction; the
fallback branch is therefore never taken.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{What each decoder buys}

The two decoders are not ordered by quality, and the trade-off between them is one
of the objects of study of this thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Independent decoding} reads each level's scores as the model
    produced them. Its advantages are that a coarse mistake does not remove the
    correct fine class from consideration, that each per-level accuracy is
    attributable to that level's head alone, and that the residual inconsistency is
    observable and therefore usable as a measurement. Its disadvantage is that it
    offers no structural guarantee at all: the predicted path may be inadmissible,
    which is a defect whenever a downstream consumer requires a valid path rather
    than three independent labels.
    \item \textbf{Top-down decoding} purchases exact consistency
    (\cref{prop:topdown_consistency}) at no computational cost and with no change to
    the trained model. Restricting the argmax to a sibling group can also correct a
    fine-level error outright when the coarse prediction is right, since the
    competing classes under other parents are removed from the comparison. What it
    pays for this is \emph{error propagation}: a mistake at a coarse level removes
    the correct fine class from the feasible set, so a sample that independent
    decoding would have classified correctly at the fine level is necessarily
    misclassified. Two further consequences matter when its numbers are read. It
    cannot change the level-$1$ prediction, so it alters only levels $l\ge2$, and
    deeper-level accuracy under it is no longer a property of that level's head
    alone but of the whole path above it. And because it forces
    $\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{topdown}}=0$ identically, it makes the consistency effect
    of any training-time mechanism invisible.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The inference grid and post-hoc evaluation}
\label{ssec:inference_grid}

The post-hoc evaluation starts from a fixed checkpoint and varies three choices:
whether HCC is applied, whether the output is read as node scores or as
taxonomy-subspace norms, and whether the resulting scores are decoded
independently or top-down. The corresponding grid is
\[
\{\text{node score},\text{subspace norm}\}
\times\{\text{no transform},\text{HCC}\}
\times\{\text{independent},\text{top-down}\},
\]
and contains eight conditions for each model family. The comparisons serve
different purposes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-hoc correction.} Applying HCC to a model trained without
          it measures the effect of correcting the output alone.
    \item \textbf{Training under the constraint.} Comparing otherwise matched
          models trained with and without HCC, under a common inference rule,
          also captures what was learned in the presence of the constraint.
    \item \textbf{Readout effect.} Changing the readout tests whether the result
          comes from the coordinates themselves or from the rule used to turn
          them into class scores.
\end{itemize}

The algebra rules out one possible effect in advance. When the readout consists
of signed node scores and decoding is top-down, post-hoc HCC cannot alter the
predicted path. It leaves the coarsest scores untouched and, at every later
level, adds a common scalar to the children of a given parent
(\cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift}). Top-down decoding compares only those siblings,
so their order is preserved. Any metric computed solely from the decoded labels
is consequently identical with and without the post-hoc projection.

The argument no longer applies once node magnitudes are used, as in Hier-COS: a
common shift need not preserve the order of absolute values. It also fails for
the subspace-norm readout because each norm combines coordinates from more than
one level. In either case, HCC can affect both independent and top-down
predictions.

In every family the transform is applied to the scores the model emits, which
are the same quantities a trained HCC arm constrains
(\S\ref{ssec:hcc_family_adaptation}). The post-hoc condition therefore performs
exactly the correction such an arm performs inside its own forward pass, and the
difference between the two isolates what training under the constraint changed
rather than a difference in what was projected.

\subsection{Model-state selection}
\label{ssec:inference_selection}

Independent and top-down decoding need not favour the same training epoch. Each
decoder therefore receives its own checkpoint, selected on the validation split
using the metrics produced by that decoder.

Candidate checkpoints are ordered lexicographically by
\[
\Big(\;\mathrm{FPA}_{\text{mode}},\;\; -\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{mode}},\;\;
\mathrm{wAP}_{\text{mode}}\;\Big)
\]
for the relevant mode. Full-path accuracy is compared first; equal values are
resolved by lower TICE and then by higher weighted AP. When hierarchical metrics
are unavailable, deepest-level accuracy is used on its own.

Accordingly, the final top-down row is evaluated from the top-down-selected
checkpoint and the independent row from the independently selected checkpoint.
The epochs may differ. Reusing one of these checkpoints for both rows would make
the decoder comparison partly a comparison of selection rules.

\subsection{Which mechanisms act at inference}
\label{ssec:inference_interaction}

Of the four training interventions, only HCC remains part of the forward
computation at inference. Lexicographic mode and the LH-projection influence how
the parameters are updated, while direct subspace supervision replaces the
training objective. Once the parameters are fixed, none of these three changes
the emitted output. HCC does: the scores are projected during both training and
evaluation. The post-hoc grid of \S\ref{ssec:inference_grid} is intended to
separate this immediate correction from the effect of having trained through
the projection.

An HCC projection should not be confused with a guarantee of path consistency.
The cascade enforces
$M_l\hat z_{l+1}=\hat z_l$, an equality in \emph{logit space} between a parent's
score and the sum of its children's. It does not imply
$\pi_l\big(\arg\max_j \hat z_{l+1}[j]\big)=\arg\max_i \hat z_l[i]$. For example,
the parent with the largest total score need not contain the single
highest-scoring child. As \cref{cor:hcc_sibling_shift} shows, HCC changes the
relative order only of classes that have different parents. It can reduce
$\mathrm{TICE}_{\text{independent}}$, but only top-down decoding forces that
quantity to zero.

\Cref{fig:mechanism_placement} and \cref{tab:framework_mechanisms} make clear
that a shared coarse-to-fine motivation does not make the methods
interchangeable. Lexicographic mode acts
on the assembled parameter update, the LH-projection on the signal returned at
a branch point, and HCC on the model's logits. None of them establishes
sequential lexicographic optimality. Direct subspace supervision belongs to a
different category altogether: it imposes no priority order, but replaces the
quantity supervised by the Hier-COS objective.

The closest pair is lexicographic mode and the LH-projection. The first projects
mini-batch loss gradients within shared parameter blocks
(\S\ref{ssec:lex_blocks}); the second uses the local Jacobian of a higher-level
head to filter a per-sample gradient at the branch point
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_construction}). Their guarantees are first-order guarantees
on an update. HCC instead satisfies an exact affine constraint on every sample's
logits, both while training and at inference, without directly resolving loss
conflicts in shared parameters.

Differentiating through HCC does filter gradients, as discussed in
\S\ref{ssec:hcc_gradient_cost}, but that effect is induced by the output
constraint and cannot be switched on independently. For this reason HCC is
classified here as an output-space method rather than an explicit
gradient-projection method. The readout and decoder form a further category:
they can be changed without retraining the model
(\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}).

\subsection{One mechanism per run}
\label{ssec:design_axis}

The training matrix uses mutually exclusive mechanism arms: a run contains no
more than one of the four interventions. This restriction combines two kinds of
case. Some pairs are incompatible with the definitions used here; others could
be trained together but are deliberately left outside the experimental scope.

Three structural incompatibilities involve the LH-projection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HCC and the LH-projection.} The LH-projection is defined by the
    way each head reads the shared representation, whereas HCC replaces the
    emitted scores with the solution of an affine problem anchored at the
    preceding level. Once HCC is introduced, the head weights used to reserve a
    row space no longer describe the complete map from representation to the
    constrained logits evaluated by the loss.
    \item \textbf{The LH-projection and lexicographic mode.} Both remove a
    higher-priority component from a lower-priority learning signal
    (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_forward}). They do so at different locations --- the
    branch point and the assembled parameter update --- but their combination
    would apply the same principle twice and obscure attribution.
    \item \textbf{The LH-projection and direct subspace supervision.} The former
    assumes that each level loss depends only on its own branch. A score
    $z_l[c]$, by contrast, is the norm of the ancestor, self and descendant
    coordinates associated with $c$, and consequently spans several levels
    (\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}(ii)).
\end{itemize}

Other pairs are well defined. HCC may, for example, be combined with
lexicographic mode. Although $\hat z_l$ depends on raw scores from preceding
levels, detaching the previous level keeps
$\nabla_\theta\ell_l(\hat z_l)$ on the level-$l$ path, so the three gradients
required by lexicographic mode remain available. Direct subspace supervision
also supplies one differentiable loss per level and can in principle accompany
either lexicographic mode or HCC.

These combinations are not trained in the present study. Apart from the added
experimental cost, their interpretation would be less clean. HCC combined with
lexicographic mode would impose coarse-to-fine structure in both output and
gradient space; \cref{prop:lex_firstorder} would still hold, but for losses
evaluated on HCC-constrained scores. Pairing HCC with direct subspace supervision
would similarly apply the subspace norm to coordinates that HCC had already
coupled. In place of joint training, the post-hoc study applies HCC to
checkpoints trained under the other arms (\S\ref{ssec:inference_grid}). This is
useful for measuring the immediate output correction, but it is not evidence
about how the two mechanisms would behave if trained together.

The mechanism axis is therefore a single choice among five conditions,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\big\{\,&\text{none},\ \text{lexicographic mode},\ \text{LH-projection},\\
&\text{direct subspace supervision},\ \text{HCC}\,\big\},
\end{aligned}
\]
with the shared \emph{none} condition providing the baseline for each
comparison.

LH-DNN is the exception to the intervention structure in
Table~\ref{tab:framework_support}. Its branch-point projection is already part of
the architecture. Lexicographic mode would repeat a similar gradient principle,
whereas HCC would replace the output structure that the baseline is meant to
evaluate. The model is therefore retained both as a baseline and as the source
of the LH-projection, but no additional mechanism arm is attached to it.

Hier-COS, by contrast, admits all four interventions and thus provides the
within-family comparison between lexicographic mode and the LH-projection. In
that comparison the backbone, frame, loss and level-specific learning rates are
matched; the associated structural ablations are described in
\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}. Direct subspace supervision is confined to this
family because it is defined on taxonomy-subspace scores
(\S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting}), which classifier-head models do not expose as a
training quantity.

HRN's restriction to three hierarchy levels comes from the published method,
not from the datasets selected for this study. It happens to match the
three-level formulation used for lexicographic mode. HCC and the LH-projection,
considered independently of HRN, are defined for arbitrary $L$.

\subsection{Requirements by model family}
\label{ssec:design_space}

The pattern in Table~\ref{tab:framework_support} is largely determined by the
decomposition of the training objective. A loss coupled across all taxonomy
nodes does not, as it stands, supply the separate gradients that either
gradient-space method requires. Whether it can be regrouped so that it does
receives three different answers here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exact regrouping (Hier-COS and HRN).} Their level terms sum to
          the original objective --- up to a parameter-independent constant in
          the Hier-COS case --- so the gradient-space arm can be compared
          against the unmodified baseline
          (\S\ref{ssec:lex_family_adaptation}).
    \item \textbf{Excluded regrouping (H-CAST).} Its global KL term also
          regroups exactly, but each resulting term would share one normaliser
          over the concatenated hierarchy, collapsing the support partition the
          experiment is meant to observe; it is therefore excluded rather than
          decomposed.
    \item \textbf{No usable regrouping (LH-DNN).} Its native objective does not
          supply the required level-wise decomposition.
\end{itemize}

A global softmax is thus still compatible with
lexicographic mode when its three level-specific terms remain available as
differentiable scalars, even though they share a normaliser. The LH-projection
demands stricter separation: each loss must be a function of its own branch, so
normalisation must be carried out within the corresponding level block
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_hiercos}).

Replacing global with level-wise normalisation, unlike an exact regrouping, does
change the objective and not merely its presentation. For that reason, the
identity frame and the per-level softmax are evaluated as structural adaptations
before the LH-projection is added. This prevents an effect caused by the revised
objective from being attributed to the projection.

The table leaves out two more specific constraints. First, the representation
used by the LH-projection must be wider than the total number of non-leaf classes
(\S\ref{ssec:lhproj_setting}). Second, direct subspace supervision assumes hard
path labels. Label smoothing, mixup and cutmix are consequently disabled for
that arm (\S\ref{ssec:subspace_setting}).

\medskip

The protocol makes two decisions. During training, a model uses one of five
conditions: the unmodified baseline, lexicographic mode, the
LH-projection, direct subspace supervision, or HCC. At evaluation, its scores
are examined under the readout and decoding rules of
\S\ref{sec:methodology_inference}, with a checkpoint selected separately for
each decoder. This design preserves the distinction between a learned effect
and an inference-time correction, while avoiding combinations whose influence
could not be attributed cleanly.

\Cref{ch:experiments} applies this methodology to the selected model families
and datasets, and defines the metrics and protocol used for the comparisons.
